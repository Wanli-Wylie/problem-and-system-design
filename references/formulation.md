## How to read this file

Each move has the same elements: a **statement** in its mature form; **why** it holds and what failure it prevents; **where it came from**, as the moves P1 to P18 of the source history; **what it is not**, the over-correction, which is part of the move because a formulation pushed one way grows the opposite failure; **signs you are violating it**; a **worked application** in a domain far from the history's; and the **kinds** of its examples.

The source history is one line of work: a declarative language for scientific computation and the system built to realize computations written in it. The moves are stated at the enduring level. An example from that history is **contextual** when it holds because of a technology and **objective-bound** when it follows from that system's goals; import the move, not the example. A quotation from that history replaces the project's name in brackets and is located as "(the owner of the source history, <date>; `cases.md` P<n>)" or "(agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P<n>)"; [cases.md](cases.md) tells each P-move with its full locators, and each move's derivation is in the research record the moves were drawn from, which [origins.md](origins.md) describes and which is not distributed.

The words problem, sub-problem and solver are used here as words for understanding. Nothing in this file says that a system should contain an object, a hierarchy or a protocol by those names.

## F1 The fixed meaning

**Statement.** A formulation is two lists: what it fixes and what it leaves free. It fixes the meaning of what is to be computed (which values, from which inputs, by which declared operations), in a representation chosen so that the structure a solver needs is exposed; it leaves free how the meaning is realized. A restriction earns its place by what the solver can derive from it and by nothing else. A computational problem has four parts: a criterion for an acceptable answer; a representation in which the situation and the permitted moves can be written; a practicable amount of computation; and a link from the representation to the world the answer must serve. Every matter has one of three homes: fixed by the formulation; bound when the environment is known (the machine a run meets, the facts a case brings); or decided during operation; nothing is moved earlier because it could be. Two guards hold the line: nothing of a solution's facts flows up into the fixed meaning, and nothing of the fixed meaning flows down to remove a solver's meaningful choice. Legality does not depend on what can execute the formulation today. The vocabulary may be closed while the meaning is left open where fixing it would fix execution.

**Why.** A formulation that fixes nothing has no solver: there is nothing to derive from. One that fixes the realization has no choices: the solver follows orders it could have improved on. Between the two, the chief act of formulation is a restriction that trades expressive freedom for structure the solver can use and freedom the realization can use. Judging each restriction by what is derived from it, not by elegance or completeness, keeps the fixed meaning small without starving it. The fourth part needs separate attention because it is the one place a representation can be wrong with no internal check noticing: a representation can be consistent, closed and efficiently solvable and still describe the wrong thing. The three homes stop matters that depend on the environment or on the course of operation from being written into the formulation because that is where the writer stood. The two guards stop the drift in both directions, upward where a solver's convenience becomes meaning, downward where meaning becomes so exact that it dictates how to execute. Separating legality from executability keeps the formulation from shrinking to whatever implementation exists first.

**Where it came from.** P1 The declarative restriction is the origin; P9, P12, P14 and P16 moved its line, and P18 reopened its standing. The founding statement chose a representation that would "deliberately restrict[s] the expressive freedom of a program so that ... computation structure, data dependencies, local computation, and communication requirements becomes explicitly exposed" (the owner of the source history, September 2026; `cases.md` P1). The three homes are the language design's criterion (agents' text of the source history, paraphrased; `cases.md` P1), stated for a declaration executed as bounded runs: the declaration, the runtime when it finds the machine, the runtime as the run goes. The upward guard came from P12 and the downward guard from P14.

**What it is not.** It is not minimalism: a construct enters when the solver must see it to choose well, and the space is bounded by what the system must do well. It is not completeness: corner cases nobody needs are excluded, not covered. It is not a set of checks: a precondition the producer vouches for can be part of the meaning without anyone verifying it at run time, and removing a check is not removing the precondition. Nor is its link to the world checked by its own tests: a check of the link between a representation and the world is not a check of a fact the system produced, and it is made by varying what the representation holds fixed and the world does not (a sample rebuilt from clean, a run repeated at doubled resolution); [B4 Facts by construction, checks at boundaries](principles.md#b4-facts-by-construction-checks-at-boundaries) draws the same line from the system's side. And it is not frozen; in the history it was revised rarely, on stated evidence, mostly by narrowing or by relocation and once by a widening the solver's need justified, and a revision of it was said to be one.

**Signs you are violating it.**
- Nobody can say in two lists what the formulation fixes and what it leaves free.
- A construct is in the fixed meaning and nothing a solver derives depends on it.
- A fact about one machine, library or compiler appears in the meaning.
- A rule of the meaning leaves the solver one way to proceed where several would compute the same thing.
- A current limitation of the implementation is written as a rule of the language.
- Nobody has asked how the representation is tied to the world it serves.

**Worked application: a compiler's intermediate representation.** A team designs the intermediate representation of an optimizing compiler for a systems language with several targets. The draft records operations in source order, the target's pointer width, a fixed evaluation order for every floating-point expression, and an operation set copied from the first target's instruction set.

Asked as F1, the draft mixes the two lists. The representation must fix the value each operation computes, the types, the data dependencies, the observable effects and their order under the language's memory model, and the preconditions the source language leaves undefined when violated. It should leave free instruction selection, register allocation, scheduling, layout and vector width. Static single assignment earns its place because def-use chains follow from it; a rule that pointers do not alias unless declared earns its place because freedom to reorder loads follows from it. Source order earns nothing the dependencies do not already give, and it removes the scheduler's choice, so it goes.

The three homes sort the rest. Pointer width depends on the machine and is bound when the target is known; in the front end's output it is a solution's fact flowing up. A fixed floating-point order belongs to the meaning only where the source language promises it; elsewhere it removes the optimizer's freedom to reassociate, and the repair is to put the latitude inside the definition, as a per-operation statement of whether reassociation is permitted. Which version of a hot loop a just-in-time tier picks is decided during operation and has no place in the representation. A fused multiply-add and a gather enter although smaller operations could express them, because the vectorizer must see them to choose well. A program using a 128-bit integer operation stays legal on a target that lacks one; that backend expands it, and the absence is a fact of the backend. Last, the link to the world: if the representation defines integer overflow to wrap while the source language leaves it undefined, optimizations the source permits are lost and every internal check still passes. The team writes that link down as an assumption to test against the language's specification.

**Kinds of example.** The two lists, the test of a restriction by what is derived from it, the three homes and the two guards are enduring. The homes' form as a declaration and its runs, the history's tensor language, its grain and where its line fell are objective-bound.

## F2 Answerability

**Statement.** The formulation states what the system is answerable for, to whom, in their words, and what the measure of the whole is. The answerability is a few guarantees, and an aim that a named party observes and relies on, with its scope and bound, is one of them, as [A1 Responsibilities before mechanisms](principles.md#a1-responsibilities-before-mechanisms) states. Otherwise the measure binds no construction. Where the system's choices are made by strategies that are meant to improve, the measure is best stated as properties of the space in which they choose (what can be expressed, what crossing between alternatives costs, what can be known when choosing), not as a score; a system with no improving strategies keeps an ordinary measure of outcome. The quality of particular choices is not the system's answerability: it belongs to the policies that fill decision points, while the system answers for the facts those policies read. Promises to a party are kept apart from the means of keeping them. A count of the formulation's own nouns is progress on nothing.

**Why.** Without a short statement of answerability, mechanisms justify one another, which A1 Responsibilities before mechanisms argues for the system; this move is where the statement is first formed, and it adds three things. A measure turned into a guarantee makes each layer grow machinery to prove it; where strategies improve, stated as properties of the choice space, the measure says what the system owes whoever chooses (alternatives, cheap crossings, knowledge) without saying which choice is right. The converse error is as costly: an aim that someone relies on with a bound (a build that finishes within a minute, a statutory deadline) is a promise, and classing it as the measure leaves it unkept. Placing the quality of choices outside the answerability lets strategies improve without the system changing. And a formulation that writes promises and means as one cannot change a means without seeming to break a promise, so means harden into promises nobody asked for.

**Where it came from.** P7 Three responsibilities cut answerability by kind of responsibility, not by package; P11 placed the quality of choices at decision points, with the system answerable for the facts they read; P16 reduced the commitments to three responsibilities; P18 restated the objective as three properties of the space in which strategies choose, "not a score" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P18), for a system whose strategies were the thing to be improved. The reading of that framing found what it still lacked: promises to those who submit work were not kept apart from the means of keeping them.

**What it is not.** It is not a list of everything the system could compute, not a score, and not a mission statement handed down as a requirement. It is not indifference to quality: quality is pursued, by strategies judged against the measure. And it changes when the objective changes.

**Signs you are violating it.**
- The formulation opens with components rather than with what anyone relies on.
- A requirement cannot be traced to a party who would notice its absence.
- The measure appears as a guarantee lower parts must demonstrate.
- The system promises good choices rather than the conditions for making them.
- Progress is reported as the number of kinds or components added.

**Worked application: a recommendation system's objective.** A shop writes the objective of its recommendation system as "maximize click-through rate". Candidate generation, the ranker, the dashboard and every team's review inherit it.

Asked as F2, the statement names no party, turns a measure into a guarantee each layer must show, and promises the quality of choices. The reformulation starts with the parties, in their words. Shoppers: what I came for can be found; I am not shown what I bought or said I do not want; what I do is not used where I opted out. Sellers: an item that matches a search can be found by those searching. Operators: pages load in time and cost per request stays within a budget a team can see. These are the guarantees. "An item marked not interested is not shown again for ninety days" is a promise; "a blocklist table in the feature store" is a means, written separately so it can become a filter in retrieval without anyone's promise changing.

The shop's ranking strategies are meant to improve, so the measure becomes long-run satisfaction, stated as properties of the space in which they choose. Can the candidate stage produce what a good strategy would want to rank, including new items? Does the cost of moving candidates from retrieval to ranking force strategies to see fewer alternatives than they could use? Are the signals that would change the expected value of showing an item available when ranking happens? A signal enters the ranker's view if it can change the legality, expected cost or future value of an action; otherwise it is collected for nothing. Click-through rate stays as one observation among several. Which item comes first for this shopper now is a ranking policy at a decision point, improvable without the system changing. And when a team reports twelve candidate generators instead of eight, F2 says that is progress on nothing until it shows as a property of the choice space.

**Kinds of example.** A few guarantees in their users' words, an aim with a party and a bound as a guarantee, and promises apart from means are enduring. The measure as properties of the choice space is objective-bound: it follows from a system whose strategies are meant to improve. The history's three responsibilities and three progress criteria are objective-bound.

## F3 Intrinsic or attached

**Statement.** For each element of a formulation, ask whether it is in the problem or attached: an assumption of some solution, a frame from a neighbouring domain, a remnant of an earlier design, or a convenience of the representation. A formulated problem is a question, a space of candidate answers, constraints and an owner, and an attached element can sit in any of them. Two tests do most of the work. The **second-implementation test**: what would an independent implementation need to behave the same at the boundaries and after loss, and what is one way of thinking the problem through written down as a requirement? The **would-it-emerge test**: built today from the behaviours the system must provide and the substrates available, would this structure appear? Narrower tests catch narrower attachments: **openness** (at least two materially different answers, or a solution in disguise?); **grounding** (does a physical fact, a library's contract or a real lesson make it hard?); **no leakage** (does it presuppose a technology, a data structure or a prior abstraction?); the **owner question** (who decides, who is constrained?); the **membership question** (a concept of the core, or local information of one part?); **provenance** (a term with no earlier history in the line of work marks an imported frame); and, for problems located in a world, the cut between requirement and domain on one side and the machine on the other: an element belongs to a solution if it can change without changing the requirement or the domain. A survey of real use sorts each use of a representation into a stable intent, a spelling shaped by some interface, or something incidental. An attached element is removed, and what it was attached to is named.

**Why.** Attached elements look like problems: same grammar, long arguments, and usually ready answers, because the solution or domain they came from already solved them. Their cost is manufactured difficulty: a frame imported from another kind of system brings that system's hard problems, and the work solves problems the system does not have. Several tests are needed because elements attach in different places. Openness and no leakage catch a solution phrased as a question; grounding catches a difficulty with nothing behind it; the owner question catches a question put to the wrong party; provenance catches vocabulary from elsewhere; the second-implementation and would-it-emerge tests catch a whole structure that is one way of thinking written as a requirement. Naming what an element was attached to matters because one attachment usually carried several elements, and they leave together.

**Where it came from.** P8 The problem space gave the tests at the scale of one problem; P9 showed what they could not reach, four frames that had entered through the shared premises; P12 gave provenance and membership their sharpest case, a term for classes of concrete shapes imported from compilers; P16 supplied the last two tests, the second in the owner's question whether, built "from the real behaviors [the system] must provide together with the native software substrates already available", the "current object structure" would "still emerge naturally" (the owner of the source history, 2026-10-06; `cases.md` P16); P17 turned the tests on requirements themselves.

**What it is not.** It is not a verdict that attached elements are wrong: each was the representation the work needed when written, and what it produced is kept as material. It is not a purge of everything borrowed: an element from a neighbouring domain is intrinsic when the problem has that property too, and grounding tells. And the tests certify nothing: they run inside the formulation's premises, and an attachment carried by a premise passes them all (F6).

**Signs you are violating it.**
- A requirement uses the vocabulary of a different kind of system.
- A question admits one answer, which is already built somewhere.
- A term appears that no earlier document in the line of work uses.
- Every option in a long option list assumes the same object.
- Removing an element would change nothing anyone relies on but would remove a mechanism.

**Worked application: a data pipeline's contract.** A platform team drafts the contract for a daily orders table used by three downstream teams: each order event is delivered exactly once; rows are sorted by event time; the schema is registered as Avro in the schema registry; the table is ready by 06:00; the pipeline is restartable from any step; a "golden record" is kept for each customer.

The second-implementation test does most of the sorting. An independent pipeline meeting what the three teams rely on would need one row per order identifier, reflecting the latest correction received before the partition closes. It would not need exactly-once delivery, which is streaming vocabulary describing one machine: an idempotent overwrite of the day's partition meets the need, and the deduplication service the phrase called for does not arise. It would not need rows sorted by event time, which no consumer relies on and which was attached to an earlier flat-file export; nor Avro in the schema registry, where what consumers need is that columns and types are versioned and a removed column is announced a fixed number of days ahead. "Ready by 06:00" stays, with its party named: the finance team's report runs at 07:00 and goes to a regulator. "Restartable from any step" looked like a requirement as well, until the owner question showed that it constrains nobody outside the producing team; it is a means of meeting 06:00 and moves to the team's runbook.

The would-it-emerge test settles the last element. Built today from what consumers rely on and what the warehouse offers, would a "golden record" store appear? It would not: consumers need only a stable customer identifier on each order, which the source already supplies, and the term came from a master-data vendor's vocabulary. The contract ends with four promises and no deduplication service, sorting step or golden-record store; the other tests had nothing to add.

**Kinds of example.** The tests, and the rule that an attached element leaves with what it was attached to named, are enduring. The frames found in the history (a database's transactions, a compiler's static shapes, a hard real-time system's bounds) are contextual.

## F4 Named in the world

**Statement.** A problem is mature when its parts can be named separately in the vocabulary of fields that already exist. Each named part is asked of the world first: does something that exists already solve it? A part the world solves dissolves, and the formulation states the reliance in one sentence. What remains is the system's own problem, usually what lies between the parts: the boundaries and coordination created when existing things are composed, which [B1 Design inside the existing world](principles.md#b1-design-inside-the-existing-world) states from the system's side. For a problem whose world is itself computational, the world is the software that already exists, and the domain's properties are that software's documented semantics.

**Why.** A part that cannot be named in an existing field cannot be checked against anything; every question about it is answered inside the formulation, which then grows concepts that explain one another. A named part can be recognized as solved, or as a known kind of hard, and either way the formulation stops carrying it alone. Asking the world first is cheaper than designing, and it makes the system's own problem visible as the residue. The residue is missed in both directions: a formulation that names nothing builds a weaker copy of what exists; one that assumes existing parts compose by themselves misses the problem only the system can own.

**Where it came from.** P7 Three responsibilities gave the maturity test; P10 made the software stacks that exist the point of departure, and guarded the residue in the same position: "the real boundaries and coordination mechanisms created between them are often precisely the things this system must own" (the owner of the source history, 2026-09-25; `cases.md` P10). When the structure was then to be derived from a survey of substrates, the owner supplied it as a premise instead, with the rule that a new need is first tried inside it. P11 recorded the result: of 416 problems re-asked of a candidate, 22 were dissolved by existing software, each dissolution stated as a reliance on a named mechanism.

**What it is not.** It is not "existing systems are the architecture": a system that uses a solver, a queue and a database is not described by them. It is not a reason to begin from a survey of substrates and let the structure emerge from their crossings; the structure is supplied as a premise and the survey tested against it. And naming a part does not promise that it dissolves; it may show a known hard problem, which is still progress.

**Signs you are violating it.**
- A part has a name nobody outside the project would recognize.
- The formulation designs what an operating system, language, library or service already provides.
- Every part is assigned to an existing system and nothing is left for the system itself.
- The structure is being derived from a list of what the substrates expose.
- A reliance on an existing mechanism is assumed and not stated.

**Worked application: a scheduling service for a hospital.** A hospital wants a service that produces nurse rosters for its wards and keeps them right through the day: sick calls, emergency admissions, a nurse who must leave. The first formulation is a "scheduling engine" holding staff, rules, preferences, shifts, notifications and history.

Named in existing fields: building a roster that meets coverage, skill mix, labour rules and preferences is the nurse rostering problem of operations research, with mature constraint and integer programming solvers. Labour rules (rest periods, consecutive nights, overtime) come from a collective agreement and the law. Time zones and daylight saving belong to standard libraries. Who is qualified for what lives in the human resources system of record. Telling a nurse about a change is messaging; the record of who changed what is an append-only table.

Asked of the world, most of this dissolves, each with its reliance stated: the search is carried by an existing solver (a four-week roster for one ward is solved within the planning window, measured on last year's data); daylight saving by the time library (shift boundaries stored in local wall time with the zone); qualifications by the human resources system, read and not copied; messaging by the service the hospital already runs.

What remains lies between the parts, and it is the service's own problem. When a nurse calls in sick at 05:30, the solver can produce a new optimal roster, but one that moves twelve people is worse than a feasible one that moves two; repairing a published roster with least disturbance is a problem the literature names and no solver decides for this hospital. The solver speaks in variables, the rules in clauses of an agreement, and the charge nurse in reasons ("she worked last night"); translating among them, both ways, is the service's. And when the solver's repair, a charge nurse's manual edit and a change from the human resources system disagree about one shift, which stands is a question none of the parts can answer. The formulation now has three problems of its own and a list of stated reliances, in place of an engine that contained everything.

**Kinds of example.** The maturity test, asking the world first, and the residue as the system's own problem are enduring. The substrate that dissolved problems in the history (operating-system processes, a consistent key-value store, an accelerator vendor's stack) is contextual.

## F5 The concrete form

**Statement.** A problem answered against concepts is not yet formulated. Ask it of a concrete object, a candidate made of things a reader can argue with. Write the problem in plain words stripped of the ontology that phrased it (a situation the software must handle, a fact it must maintain, a boundary it must observe, a failure it must propagate, a choice it must leave to someone); find where it exists in the object; if nowhere, ask what in the old form produced it. Each inherited problem meets a fate: **dissolved** by the world (state the reliance), **carried** by an existing element, **expands** the design, **does not arise** (state the condition under which it would), **the owner's** (naming which holder of the meaning), or **outside**. Where meaning is held by several parties or outside the organization (a statute, a contract, a field's conventions), the formulation names each holder and how its changes reach the system; the source history had one owner, and this sentence is drawn from cases applied after it ([cases.md](cases.md), the trial). A fate with no member is not a gap. The record for one problem is: as posed; where it exists; fate; answer; goes to; open. [J18 The fates](judgment.md#j18-the-fates) keeps the same fates; where the two lists diverge, the names here govern, since a fate is a finding about the problem.

**Why.** Without an object, problems can only explain one another: a question about a general model is answered by another concept, which raises its own questions. With an object, a question either lives somewhere in it and can be answered there, or does not, and its absence is information. Three of the six fates cannot be stated without an object at all. The plain-words step matters because an inherited problem arrives in the vocabulary of the formulation that raised it, and in that vocabulary it always has a place. The fates keep apart findings with different consequences: "the world answers it" creates a reliance the design must state; "it does not arise" creates a boundary of applicability. Merging them loses one.

**Where it came from.** P10 is the failure that taught it: a closed model gave every problem an answer, and the answers explained one another. P11 The fates is the move, in the owner's operation: "In the current candidate software system, in what concrete form does the problem that [the earlier model] was trying to address actually exist?" (the owner of the source history, 2026-09-26; `cases.md` P11). Of 416 inherited problems, 29 did not arise, nine of them resting on one structural fact, a single decider per run. The same direction barred forced one-to-one correspondences, and an earlier one barred pilot-driven development (P10, P11).

**What it is not.** It is not a coverage exercise: the object owes no element to each inherited concept, and a problem that does not arise is a finding, not a gap. It is not a pilot whose shape becomes the architecture: the object is a reference chosen for its completeness as a candidate, not a minimal example from which the architecture grows. A probe or pilot campaign that measures the world (how a criterion behaves, what a run costs) is part of forming the problem; [D1 A concrete object early](principles.md#d1-a-concrete-object-early) has the same clause. And nothing in the object is sacred because it exists; a problem that expands the design changes it.

**Signs you are violating it.**
- Questions are answered with new concepts, and each raises more questions.
- Nobody can point to where in a candidate a problem lives.
- An inherited problem is kept because it was on the list.
- Every inherited concept has been mapped to a component one to one.
- A "does not arise" is recorded without the condition under which it would.

**Worked application: a game engine's physics step.** A studio builds the physics for a single-player action game. An earlier design document for a general simulation framework lists problems the physics must solve: deterministic replay across machines; fast objects tunnelling through thin walls; constraint resolution order; sleeping resting bodies; transactional rollback of a step when the solver diverges; units and scale; network synchronization of authoritative state; allocation during a step; and safety when gameplay code mutates the world mid-step.

The concrete object is a candidate step: a fixed sixty-hertz loop that integrates velocities, finds candidate pairs by sweep and prune, computes contacts, runs a sequential impulse solver for eight iterations, integrates positions and puts quiet islands to sleep; gameplay scripts run between steps, not inside one. Each problem is asked of it in plain words.

Tunnelling is a situation the software must handle and nothing handles it: it **expands** the design, with continuous collision detection for bodies flagged fast. Constraint order is **carried** by the solver's iteration by body index, with a precision added (stable across runs on one machine). Sleeping is **carried** by the island step. Allocation is **dissolved** by the engine's frame arena, with the reliance stated (reset once per frame; physics allocates only from it). Scale is **the owner's**: art direction holds it, and the physics documents the range its tolerances are tuned for.

Cross-machine determinism was first filed as expanding the design, and the team began to specify fixed-point arithmetic for the solver. Asked where it exists in the object, it was nowhere: replays are stored as recorded state, not as inputs, so nothing is re-simulated on another machine. It **does not arise**, and the record gives the condition under which it would: lockstep multiplayer, or replays stored as inputs. Network synchronization does not arise under the same condition. Transactional rollback was a database's frame in a world that only moves forward; a diverging solver is clamped and the step proceeds, and the problem would arise with rollback networking, which re-simulates past frames. Mid-step mutation does not arise because scripts run between steps; it would arise if contact callbacks could change the world during solver iterations, and the record says so, so that whoever adds such callbacks finds the problem waiting. No problem went **outside**, and that fate stays empty.

**Kinds of example.** Asking inherited problems of a concrete object, the plain-words step and the six fates are enduring. The history's counts are objective-bound: the share that did not arise measures that candidate's premises as much as the problems.

## F6 The map

**Statement.** Every way of holding a problem space (a list, a register, a model, a ranking, a status) is a representation of the problem, and the work happens inside one. Ask which one you are in, what its premises are, and what it cannot show. A map of the problem space is a lens to query and a coverage instrument, never a parts list to build. Its leaves are meant to be reformulated as the object is understood. Its premises are its blind spot, and tests run inside it cannot test it. Agreement among independent cuts says where a problem is, not that it is one. A closure over the set certifies relations among the answers, not that the set's ontology is the solution's. Local correctness is not global correctness. Write the assumptions no section checks, so that a later reader can tell a failed assumption from a failed detail; write where the formulation cannot express something; treat "no precedent" as a mark of where the formulation is newest.

**Why.** A map is needed: without one, coverage cannot be seen and problems are rediscovered. But a representation is wrong where it is most convenient, and a problem space is wrong in a characteristic way: each leaf can be well formed, tested and answered while the premises all leaves share are false, because each leaf's tests take the premises as given. Several cuts agreeing does not remove this when they share inputs. A closed model is the extreme case: when every question is answered consistently, the model looks finished, but consistency among answers is a property of the representation. Writing the unchecked assumptions down is what lets the representation be wrong without the work being lost: a later failure can be traced to the assumption it falsified instead of being repaired detail by detail.

**Where it came from.** P8 The problem space built such a map deliberately, with five independent cuts and five tests per problem, and wrote its own first blind spot: the premise set itself. P9 confirmed it within a day: the manufactured problems the owner ruled on had entered through the premises, and 77 of 332 sub-questions changed (the amendment's dispositions under the owner's rulings of 2026-09-24; `cases.md` P9). P10 is the closed case: a model of 461 rows passed every check of its own, and the owner judged the kind of object, not a failed check; it was "better understood as a semantic reference map, a map of the problem space, and an instrument for scrutiny" (the owner of the source history, 2026-09-25; `cases.md` P10). A language design that closed with a list of its assumptions found that the list predicted where it later met its limits (P1).

**What it is not.** It is not a reason to have no map: the map is kept beside the work, queried for coverage, and its standing changed (from specification to map) without rewriting its text. It does not say agreement among cuts means nothing; it locates problems well. And it does not require the map to be correct before work proceeds; the object corrects the map.

**Signs you are violating it.**
- Each entry of a problem list has, or is planned to have, a component.
- An assumptions section says "none".
- Every question has an answer and the answers mostly cite other answers.
- A ranking is treated as settled because several cuts agree.
- A status label is trusted without checking which question it answers.

**Worked application: a static analyser.** A team building an analyser for null dereferences and resource leaks writes a register of 120 problems, cut five ways (language feature, analysis phase, developer workflow, performance, soundness obligations), each tested for openness and grounding. All five cuts rank alias precision hardest. The premises, stated once at the top: the analysis is sound (no missed defect of the covered kinds), whole-program, and identical across runs.

Asked as F6, the register's blind spot is its premises. None of its 120 tests could ask whether soundness is what users rely on. The users are developers reading warnings in code review, and they rely on a warning being worth reading; a sound analyser with a high false-positive rate is switched off within weeks while every problem in the register has been answered well. That is local correctness without global correctness, invisible from inside. The agreement on alias precision is evidence about where difficulty sits given soundness; replace the premise with "report only what can be explained as a path a developer can follow", and alias precision drops, while a problem the register did not hold (a short, readable trace per warning) becomes central.

The team keeps the register with its standing changed: it checks coverage when the incremental mode is designed, and it is not a list of components. Its leaves are rewritten as the analyser is understood. It gains a section of assumptions nothing in it checks: developers act on a warning within the review where it appears; the build exposes its compile commands; most code is in one language. A survey finds no existing analyser that is whole-program sound at this scale with few false positives, and the team marks that as where its formulation is newest and most likely wrong.

**Kinds of example.** Holding a problem space as a lens, the premises as its blind spot, and the written list of unchecked assumptions are enduring. The history's cuts and counts are objective-bound.

## F7 Decided absences

**Statement.** Every formulation has absences. Ask which are decisions and which are simply not yet reached, and sort them: a decided absence is stated once, with what it costs; an unreached one is left unstated. Which forms, environments or capabilities come first is progression, not architecture. That an absent capability is not a prohibition, and a possible need not a reason to carry complexity now, is [C4 Expand the platform, not the special case](principles.md#c4-expand-the-platform-not-the-special-case), and stating each decided absence once is [D5 The design explains itself](principles.md#d5-the-design-explains-itself); this move adds the sorting and the cost. Substantial changes are appropriate when they genuinely expand what the system can do or improve its organization.

**Why.** Absences drift two ways. An absence merely not reached gets written as a rule, and the rule shapes the design: interfaces close against it, data models omit what it would need, and adding it later means removing a prohibition nobody decided. Or a possible need is built into the core now, and every change pays for a capability nobody uses. Both come from treating the order in which things will be built as part of what the system is. Stating only the decided absences separates the two: a decided absence is something users rely on; an unreached one is a fact about a build. The last sentence guards the other side: a statement of decided absences is not a reason to keep the formulation small for its own sake.

**Where it came from.** P9 made it a rule, in the ruling that moved scope questions ("is X in scope at all", "which form first") from the architecture to the project's progression: "The absence of a capability from the first implementation must not be misrepresented as an architectural prohibition" (the owner of the source history, 2026-09-24; `cases.md` P9). The agents admitted a disposition (paraphrased): a question whose only motive is a possible future need may be closed by recording that the core carries nothing for it until a need appears. P14 applied the rule to the language, and P18 guarded the opposite error, minimal implementation as the objective.

**What it is not.** It is not deferral of building: what is needed now is built now, in a large coherent step if that is what it takes. It is not minimal implementation as the objective. It is not silence about decided absences: where users rely on something not being done, the formulation says so once. And it is not a list of everything the system does not do; such lists turn into prohibitions.

**Signs you are violating it.**
- "Does not support" statements that nobody decided and nobody relies on.
- An interface shaped to exclude something merely not built yet.
- A general mechanism in the core for needs no current user has.
- A roadmap written into the architecture.
- A change that would widen what the system can express refused because it is large.

**Worked application: a tax calculation library.** A library computes income tax for individuals in several jurisdictions for payroll products. Its documentation says it does not support three jurisdictions, does not handle amendments to prior years, does not let callers choose a rounding mode, does not estimate unreported income, and has no plugin system for arbitrary tax regimes.

Two of these are decisions users rely on. The library does not estimate unreported income because payroll products rely on every figure tracing to an input and a statute. Callers cannot choose rounding because each statute fixes its own, and the library's meaning includes it. Both stay, stated once with reason and cost (a caller wanting a projection does it outside).

The other three are not decisions. The missing jurisdictions are progression, but the absence has leaked into the interface through an enumeration of supported jurisdictions in the public types, so adding one is an incompatible change; the repair makes the jurisdiction a code the library looks up, with "not supported in this version" as an answer, so the absence is a fact of a release. Prior-year amendments are not reached, but asking what they would need (the tax year as an explicit input everywhere, instead of the current year implied) shows that making the year explicit widens what the library can express (amendments, back-dated corrections, projections) and simplifies its organization: worth doing now, on its merits. The plugin system serves a possible need with no current user and is not built; the jurisdiction modules sit behind one contract instead, so a new jurisdiction widens the foundation rather than adding a case.

**Kinds of example.** Separating decided absences from unreached ones, and refusing both the prohibition and the speculative mechanism, are enduring. The history's scope questions (deployment forms, hosting forms, dynamic participants) are contextual.

## How the seven make one act

The seven moves are one act seen seven ways: separating what a problem is from what was carried into its statement. F1 fixes the meaning and frees the rest, and F2 says what the system answers for and how the whole is judged; they are what the rest stands on, and [SKILL.md](../SKILL.md#how-the-two-sides-join) gives their joins with A1, A3 and B4. F3 and F4 clear away what was attached and what the world already carries, leaving the system's own problem. F5 asks what remains of a concrete object, where the problem stops explaining itself. F6 and F7 hold the formulation's own representation at a distance: the map as a lens, and a statement of decided absences saying which absences are choices.

They are not a sequence. A fate under F5 can show a premise of F1 wrong; a dissolution under F4 can remove a promise under F2; a test under F3 can find that the map of F6 carried the frame. When a later move shows an earlier premise wrong, the formulation is revised there, with a record, as [reformulation.md](reformulation.md) describes. Where the formulation's sub-problems are of one kind, the next questions are those of [recursion.md](recursion.md).
