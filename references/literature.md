# Literature

Where the methodology stands among established writing on system design, on problem formulation, representation and reformulation, and on recursion, decomposition and the reuse of mechanisms: what each source claims, where the sources disagree, and where the methodology restates, sharpens or departs. Open it to learn whether a principle or a move is new, when someone cites one of these sources against a design or a formulation, or when a tension between principles looks like one the literature already argues.

The sources were read twice: on 2026-10-09 those on system design, and on 2026-10-10, by two readers, those on problems and on recursion. Each marked on its own scale, [T] and [M] the first, [V], [E] and [S] the second, [U] both; a source read in both carries both marks.

| Mark | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **[T]** | The primary text was read; the claim and citation come from it. |
| **[M]** | The bibliographic facts and the gist were confirmed from a canonical metadata page or several consistent secondary sources; the primary text was not read. |
| **[V]** | Verified in primary text: the reader read the source itself, or the named part of it ("in part"). |
| **[E]** | Verified in an excerpt: the reader read the passage as quoted in a work read in full, or as returned by a fetch tool. Good for the quoted lines only. |
| **[S]** | Secondary: bibliographic facts and gist from summaries or citations. |
| **[U]** | Not verified; the detail rests on the reader's prior knowledge and is a pointer, not a fact. |

Quotations appear only for sources marked [T], [V] or [E]. In the first reading these were Parnas 1972, Saltzer, Reed and Clark, Moseley and Marks, the exokernel abstract, Conway, Hyrum's law, Foote and Yoder, Fowler's two essays, Kroah-Hartman, Dijkstra's EWD447 and Minsky; everything else in that reading is paraphrase, and the entry says the source was not read in primary text. Daniel Jackson writes on concepts and Michael Jackson on problem frames; "Jackson" alone under F, R, M and L is the second.

Where an entry names the source history (I³, the system the methodology was drawn from, or the pre-project workspace before it, axle in locators), it is an example; the cases are in [cases.md](cases.md).

## Sources

Each entry of the first reading gives the claim, the citation, the mark, and the principle or move ([principles.md](principles.md), [judgment.md](judgment.md)) it bears on. The second reading's sources are listed in the last two clusters, each line giving the citation, the mark and, after the semicolon, the moves it bears on; they are quoted under F, R, M and L below. Sources read in both carry both uses.

### How complex systems are structured

**Simon, near-decomposability. [M] in the first reading; [V] in the second.** Complexity in nature mostly takes hierarchic form, because hierarchies are stable and evolve faster; interactions inside a subsystem are strong and between subsystems weak. The watchmaker parable argues that stable intermediate assemblies make construction survivable. The first reading worked from secondary summaries; the second read the primary text, quoted under R below. *Source:* Herbert A. Simon, "The Architecture of Complexity", *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society* 106(6), December 1962, pp. 467-482; reprinted in *The Sciences of the Artificial* (MIT Press, 1969, later editions). *Bears on:* C1 · Closure at each scale. Simon says nothing about how to find the weak interactions in a design not yet built; D1 and D2 are the methodology's answer. In the second reading: R1, R5, R7.

**Alexander, form, context and fit; "A City is Not a Tree". [M] for the 1964 book in the first reading and [S] in the second; [V] for the 1965 essay in the second.** Design makes a form that fits a context; the designer's picture of the context is usually wrong; fit is reached by finding and removing misfits, piecemeal. The book decomposes a problem into a tree by clustering requirements, a tree Alexander later rejected in "A City is Not a Tree" (1965; [U] in the first reading; read in the second, [V], and quoted under F and R below). The book was not read in primary text. *Sources:* Christopher Alexander, *Notes on the Synthesis of Form* (Harvard University Press, 1964; the 1964 publisher was not confirmed directly, and a catalogue entry gives 1971, likely a reprint); "A City is Not a Tree", *Architectural Forum* 122(1-2), 1965. *Bears on:* A2 · Effectiveness over completeness (misfit as the unit of evidence); C1's "not one tree". In the second reading the book, with Newell and Simon, Kaplan and Simon and Marr (below), bears on F1, F6 and L3, and the essay on R4, R7 and F6.

**Parnas, decomposition criteria and information hiding. [T] in the first reading; [V] in the second.** The effectiveness of a modularization "is dependent upon the criteria used in dividing the system into modules". Dividing by processing steps is the conventional decomposition; dividing so that each module hides "a difficult design decision or a design decision which is likely to change" is better, and the resulting modules "no longer correspond to steps in the processing". The paper notes the second decomposition costs efficiency if modules are subroutines, and suggests how to avoid it. *Source:* D. L. Parnas, "On the Criteria To Be Used in Decomposing Systems into Modules", *Communications of the ACM* 15(12), December 1972; read from https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/edu/2ip30/references/criteria_for_modularization.pdf. *Bears on:* C2 · Boundaries from responsibility, not symmetry; C3 · Distinctions as fine as their consumers. In the second reading: R4, for the independence of hierarchy and clean decomposition (under R below).

**Parnas, extension and contraction. [M]** Software should be designed so that useful subsets and supersets are cheap; the critical structural choice is the "uses" relation, which when hierarchical lets subsets exist and be tested. Not read in primary text. *Source:* D. L. Parnas, "Designing Software for Ease of Extension and Contraction", *IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering* SE-5(2), March 1979, pp. 128-138 (first presented at ICSE 1978). *Bears on:* C4 · Expand the platform, not the special case; J8's truncation test asks a similar question of layers.

**Baldwin and Clark, modularity as options. [M]** Modules hidden behind design rules allow independent experiments; a module's value is its option value, less the cost of establishing the modular architecture. Not read in primary text. *Source:* Carliss Baldwin and Kim Clark, *Design Rules, Volume 1: The Power of Modularity* (MIT Press, 2000). *Bears on:* C4: an economic account of when a foundation is worth building and when it is not.

**Conway's law. [T]** "Organizations which design systems (in the broad sense used here) are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations." The page notes the name came later. *Source:* Melvin E. Conway, "How Do Committees Invent?", *Datamation*, April 1968; read at https://www.melconway.com/Home/Committees_Paper.html. *Bears on:* D6 · Escalate by semantic consequence and the working method: work is divided by responsibility boundary, not by directory, because the division reappears in the system.

**Dijkstra, separation of concerns. [T]** Study one aspect of a subject in isolation without ignoring the others; Dijkstra calls it "the only available technique for effective ordering of one's thoughts". *Source:* E. W. Dijkstra, "On the role of scientific thought" (EWD447), 30 August 1974; https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~EWD/transcriptions/EWD04xx/EWD447.html. *Bears on:* B2 · A concept is not an object: separation is a technique of thought, and does not by itself license a separate runtime, lifecycle or registry.

### Complexity, simplicity and what to build

**Brooks, essential and accidental difficulty. [M] in the first reading; [V] in the second.** The hard part of software is the specification, design and testing of the conceptual construct (its essence), not its representation (accident); no single development promises an order-of-magnitude gain. The list of inherent properties (complexity, conformity, changeability, invisibility) is from the first reader's recall, [U] at the quotation level. The first reading did not read the primary text; the second did, quoted under F, M and L below. *Source:* Frederick P. Brooks Jr., "No Silver Bullet: Essence and Accident in Software Engineering", Information Processing 1986 (IFIP), reprinted in *Computer*, April 1987, and in the 1995 anniversary edition of *The Mythical Man-Month* (dates vary between sources because of the two printings). *Bears on:* A2; the procedure for telling essence from accident (below, under Sharpens). In the second reading: F3, M4, L3.

**Moseley and Marks, "Out of the Tar Pit". [T]** "Complexity is the single major difficulty in the successful development of large-scale software systems." They adopt Brooks's distinction but "disagree with his premise that most complexity remaining in contemporary systems is essential". The main contributors they name are state, code volume and explicit control flow; the remedy is to separate essential state and logic from accidental state and control. The reader read the abstract, introduction and section headings, not the whole paper. *Source:* Ben Moseley and Peter Marks, "Out of the Tar Pit", 6 February 2006 (the SPA/BCS workshop version); https://curtclifton.net/papers/MoseleyMarks06a.pdf. *Bears on:* B3 · One fact, one owner and B4. Their own cure is a heavy architecture, a caution on the other side.

**Hickey, "Simple Made Easy". [M]** Simple means unentangled, easy means familiar; choosing the easy path accumulates complexity; to "complect" is to braid concerns together. Not read in primary text (page metadata and summary only). *Source:* Rich Hickey, "Simple Made Easy", Strange Loop, 20 October 2011, https://www.infoq.com/presentations/Simple-Made-Easy/. *Bears on:* C2 and the failure pattern *the second axis*: simplicity counted in entanglements, not parts.

**Ousterhout, *A Philosophy of Software Design*. [M]** Complexity is what makes a system hard to understand and modify (the framing of change amplification, cognitive load and unknown unknowns is from the reader's memory, [U] on wording). Good modules are deep: a simple interface over substantial function. "Define errors out of existence" removes exceptions by redefining semantics. Strategic programming invests continuously in design. Not read in primary text; summaries only. *Source:* John Ousterhout, *A Philosophy of Software Design* (Yaknyam Press, 1st ed. 2018, 2nd ed. July 2021). *Bears on:* B5 · One path for loss; C2.

**Gall's law. [M]** A complex system that works has evolved from a simple system that worked; a complex system designed from scratch does not work and cannot be patched into working; one starts over with a working simple system. Not read in primary text (the Wikipedia statement was read, not the book). *Source:* John Gall, *Systemantics* (1975 as *General Systemantics*; 1977 retitled), p. 71 per Wikipedia. *Bears on:* the tension with D1 and C4, below.

**Brooks, second-system effect and conceptual integrity. [M]** An architect's second system tends to be bloated with the ideas the first held back. Conceptual integrity, one coherent set of design ideas, is the most important consideration in system design and comes from one or a few minds (reader's recall, [U] on wording). *The Design of Design* revisits it with propriety, orthogonality and generality (per a secondary summary). Not read in primary text. *Sources:* Brooks, *The Mythical Man-Month* (Addison-Wesley, 1975; anniversary edition 1995); *The Design of Design: Essays from a Computer Scientist* (Addison-Wesley, 2010). *Bears on:* C4's warning against generalizing everything a first system lacked; D6 and the single spine of the working method.

**Fowler, YAGNI. [T]** A presumptive feature has four costs: build, delay, carry, repair. YAGNI does not apply to work that makes code easier to change, which depends on enabling practices; "Yagni is not a justification for neglecting the health of your code base". It applies when a change adds complexity now. *Source:* Martin Fowler, "Yagni", bliki, https://martinfowler.com/bliki/Yagni.html (publication date not shown). *Bears on:* C4: a capability-specific mechanism waits for need; a foundation that keeps change cheap does not.

**Fowler, sacrificial architecture. [T]** Some of the most valuable code is code you expect to replace within a few years, and building it so is a planned choice. Fowler quotes Jeff Dean: "design for ~10X growth, but plan to rewrite before ~100X". It requires internal quality high enough that modules can be replaced. *Source:* Martin Fowler, "Sacrificial Architecture", bliki, https://martinfowler.com/bliki/SacrificialArchitecture.html. *Bears on:* D4 · Revise at the level of premises: structural revision is normal work.

**Foote and Yoder, "Big Ball of Mud". [T]** The most common de facto architecture is the Big Ball of Mud, driven by expediency, deadlines, cost, skill variance, changing requirements and scale. Related patterns: Throwaway Code, Piecemeal Growth, Keep It Working, Sweeping It Under the Rug, Reconstruction. A messy start can be reasonable while the domain is poorly understood. The reader read about 100,000 of 125,000 characters. *Source:* Brian Foote and Joseph Yoder, "Big Ball of Mud", PLoP '97, Monticello, Illinois, September 1997; in *Pattern Languages of Program Design 4* (Addison-Wesley, 2000); http://www.laputan.org/mud/. *Bears on:* C1 (an architecture without closure); D4 (Reconstruction resembles rewriting a design from its premises).

**Gabriel, "Worse Is Better". [M]** Two styles: "the right thing", which favours consistency and completeness, and "worse is better", which ranks implementation simplicity first and gives up completeness and interface consistency; the second is written faster, ports more widely and spreads. The essay is ambivalent. Not read in primary text (a search summary only). *Source:* Richard P. Gabriel, "The Rise of 'Worse is Better'", from "Lisp: Good News, Bad News, How to Win Big", 1989/1990 (dates differ between reprints). *Bears on:* A2 and C4.

### Where functions go and what to expose

**Saltzer, Reed and Clark, the end-to-end argument. [T]** "The function in question can completely and correctly be implemented only with the knowledge and help of the application standing at the end points of the communication system. Therefore, providing that questioned function as a feature of the communication system itself is not possible." Low-level mechanisms for such functions "are justified only as performance enhancements". The paper opens: "Choosing the proper boundaries between functions is perhaps the primary activity of the computer system designer." *Source:* J. H. Saltzer, D. P. Reed, D. D. Clark, "End-to-End Arguments in System Design", *ACM Transactions on Computer Systems* 2(4), November 1984 (the PDF read is the MIT Laboratory for Computer Science version; the venue is from the reader's knowledge, [U]); https://web.mit.edu/Saltzer/www/publications/endtoend/endtoend.pdf. *Bears on:* B1 (a system owns exactly the knowledge only it has); B4; J17 Decision placement.

**Lampson, "Hints for Computer System Design". [M]** Hints from experience on several systems, grouped by what they serve (functionality, speed, fault tolerance); one, that making a facility fast serves clients better than making it general or powerful, was confirmed from a secondary source; the others are from the reader's recall, [U] at the wording level. Not read in primary text. *Source:* Butler W. Lampson, "Hints for Computer System Design", *ACM Operating Systems Review* 15(5), October 1983, pp. 33-48 (9th SOSP); revised in *IEEE Software* 1(1), January 1984, pp. 11-28. *Bears on:* A3; and the form: hints, explicitly not rules, which is the form this Skill takes.

**The exokernel. [T]** "Traditional operating systems limit the performance, flexibility, and functionality of applications by fixing the interface and implementation of operating system abstractions." In an exokernel "a small kernel securely exports all hardware resources through a lowlevel interface to untrusted library operating systems", which implement objects and policy; the "separation of resource protection from management" permits customization. *Source:* Dawson R. Engler, M. Frans Kaashoek, James O'Toole Jr., "Exokernel: An Operating System Architecture for Application-Level Resource Management", SOSP 1995 (venue and year from the reader's knowledge, [U]; the PDF shows authors and abstract); https://pdos.csail.mit.edu/6.828/2008/readings/engler95exokernel.pdf. *Bears on:* A3 · Fix meaning, free execution; B4 (guard what only you can guard, expose the rest).

**"Mechanism, not policy" and the X Window System. [M]** The X protocol provides mechanism, not policy, leaving interface policy to clients. Not read in primary text. *Sources:* R. W. Scheifler and J. Gettys, "The X Window System", *ACM Transactions on Graphics* 5(2), 1986 (venue from memory, [U]); RFC 1013 (1987), the sentence attributed by a search summary, not read. *Bears on:* A3.

**The Unix philosophy. [M]** Programs do one thing well, work together, and use text streams as a universal interface; build new tools rather than complicate old ones; the power of the environment comes from the relationships among programs (Kernighan and Pike). Not read in primary text; the 1978 wording is [U]. *Sources:* M. D. McIlroy, E. N. Pinson, B. A. Tague, foreword to the Unix issue, *Bell System Technical Journal* 57(6) part 2, July-August 1978; Kernighan and Pike, *The UNIX Programming Environment* (1984). *Bears on:* C2, and the tension over platform size.

**Linux norms: no stable internal API; do not break userspace. [T] for the first; [M] for the second.** Kroah-Hartman: Linux has no stable in-kernel API or binary interface; maintainers update every in-kernel caller at once; unused interfaces are removed; drivers belong in the tree. "You think you want a stable kernel interface, but you really do not." The kernel-to-userspace interface, by contrast, is very stable: Torvalds's rule, read only as quoted by search listings, is that a kernel which breaks userspace has regressed even when the root cause lies in userspace. *Sources:* Greg Kroah-Hartman, "The Linux Kernel Driver Interface" (stable-api-nonsense), https://docs.kernel.org/process/stable-api-nonsense.html (read); the regression documents and mailing-list threads were not retrieved. *Bears on:* B4 (where compatibility effort belongs); A3 (what a boundary form freezes).

**Hyrum's law. [T]** "With a sufficient number of users of an API, it does not matter what you promise in the contract: all observable behaviors of your system will be depended on by somebody." Observed by Hyrum Wright; named by Titus Winters. *Source:* https://www.hyrumslaw.com/. *Bears on:* A3 (freedom is the default inside, so commit only what crosses a boundary); C3.

**Postel's robustness principle and its critique. [M]** Be conservative in what you send and liberal in what you accept. The IAB's RFC 9413 (Thomson and Schinazi, June 2023) argues that the principle, as practised, harms interoperability over time, since tolerated flaws become entrenched and every implementation must replicate them, and recommends active protocol maintenance. Not read in primary text. *Sources:* attributed to RFC 761 (1980) by secondary sources, not read; RFC 9413, "Maintaining Robust Protocols", https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/rfc9413/ (abstract and introduction as summarized by search). *Bears on:* B4, where the methodology departs (below).

### Understanding, evolution and decay

**Naur, "Programming as Theory Building". [M]** A program expresses a theory held by its builders; the theory cannot be fully recovered from code and documentation; when the team holding it disperses the program effectively dies, though it may keep running, and later changes made without the theory degrade it. Not read in primary text (the copy is a scanned image). *Source:* Peter Naur, "Programming as Theory Building", *Microprocessing and Microprogramming* 15(5), 1985, pp. 253-261 (venue from memory, [U]). *Bears on:* D5 · The design explains itself; and the limit of what any written methodology, including this Skill, can transmit.

**Lehman's laws of software evolution. [M]** Systems embedded in a real-world context must continually change or become less satisfactory; complexity grows unless work is done to reduce it; functional content keeps growing; evolution is a multi-level feedback system. Empirical support is mixed. Not read in primary text (a Wikipedia list was read). *Sources:* M. M. Lehman, "Programs, Life Cycles, and Laws of Software Evolution", *Proceedings of the IEEE* 68(9), 1980, pp. 1060-1076; Lehman et al., "Metrics and laws of software evolution: the nineties view", METRICS '97, pp. 20-32; Herraiz et al., "The evolution of the laws of software evolution", *ACM Computing Surveys* 46(2), 2013. *Bears on:* D4: reorganization and removal are routine.

### Concepts and impossible states

**Daniel Jackson, concepts. [M]** A software system can be viewed as a collection of interacting concepts, each with a purpose and an operational principle, organized around user-visible purposes. Only the author, year and the "collection of interacting concepts" framing were confirmed; the rest is the reader's recall, [U]. Not read in primary text. *Source:* Daniel Jackson, *The Essence of Software: Why Concepts Matter for Great Design* (Princeton University Press, 2021). *Bears on:* B2: a concept earns independence by what it is for, not by its name.

**Liskov substitution. [M]** A subtype must preserve every property provable of its supertype; the notion is defined over specifications. Not read in primary text. *Source:* Barbara H. Liskov and Jeannette M. Wing, "A Behavioral Notion of Subtyping", *ACM TOPLAS* 16(6), November 1994, pp. 1811-1841, doi:10.1145/197320.197383; informal precursor, Liskov's 1987 keynote "Data Abstraction and Hierarchy". *Bears on:* C4: extension that does not invalidate what depends on the foundation.

**"Make illegal states unrepresentable". [T, partly]** In a language with variant types, model state so that invalid combinations cannot be constructed. The post read gives only the heading, "Make illegal states unrepresentable", and a before-and-after example: a connection record holding fields for states it is not in, against separate types per state. The principle's elaboration and any earlier source were not verified. *Source:* Yaron Minsky, "Effective ML Revisited", Jane Street blog, 9 March 2011, https://blog.janestreet.com/effective-ml-revisited/. *Bears on:* B4 · Facts by construction, checks at boundaries; its precondition, a representation able to carry the constraint, is contextual.

**Chesterton's fence. [M]** A reformer who finds a fence across a road and does not know its use should not clear it until he can say what it is for. Not read in primary text. *Source:* G. K. Chesterton, *The Thing* (1929), chapter "The Drift from Domesticity" (p. 35 per one secondary citation, unverified). *Bears on:* J9 The carrier rule: a removal states the function the thing served and what carries it now, not that it looks redundant.

## Tensions in the literature

The sources on system design disagree along twelve lines. The methodology takes a position on each, stated here once with the principle that holds it; the reasoning is in [principles.md](principles.md).

| # | Tension | One side | Other side | How the methodology holds it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| T1 | Defensive checking or defining errors away | Postel's tolerance; checks layered as defence in depth | Ousterhout; Minsky; end-to-end, where lower-layer checks are at best optimizations | Checks sit at trust boundaries; inside one, facts hold by construction and a disagreement is a bug that fails loudly (B4). Minsky's form needs a representation that can carry the constraint, which a process or network boundary often lacks; that is where B4 keeps its checks. |
| T2 | Minimalism or platform | Gall, YAGNI, worse is better, Unix, sacrificial architecture | Baldwin and Clark, Parnas 1979, the exokernel, Lampson; the second-system effect warns the other way | The foundation is extended in what it can express; mechanisms are not added for hypothetical failures or for uniformity (C4 with A2). See Gall below. |
| T3 | Abstraction as restriction or as extension | Parnas 1972, Liskov, Minsky narrow what clients depend on | The exokernel and "mechanism, not policy" widen what clients can do | Both, at different places: fix what crosses a real boundary and its meaning; leave execution free (A3). Hyrum's law is why the first set is kept small. |
| T4 | Small modules or deep modules | Unix, Hickey, the Clean Code style | Ousterhout | The methodology counts axes and owners, not pieces or depth: a boundary exists where ownership, execution or trust changes (C2, J2), and a second classification dimension is the cost to watch (the second axis). |
| T5 | Stable interfaces or freedom to change | Do not break userspace; Hyrum; Postel | No stable internal API; sacrificial architecture; Lehman | The kernel's own resolution: freeze what users who cannot be changed together depend on, keep free what can be changed together (B4, A3). The source history's owner ruled out compatibility between releases "for now", an objective-bound choice. |
| T6 | Essential or accidental complexity | Brooks: most remaining complexity is essential | Moseley and Marks: most is accidental | A procedure, below. |
| T7 | One mind or distributed design | Conceptual integrity from one or a few minds | Conway; distributed kernel maintenance | Integrity is kept by one spine that fixes names and positions, writers who write against it, one cross-reading, and whoever owns the system's meaning fixing the principled world while others complete the engineering (D6). Work is divided by responsibility, because Conway's law applies to agents too. |
| T8 | Decompose by process step or by decision | Flowchart decomposition | Hide decisions likely to change | Parnas's resolution, extended: the unit of extension and the axis of a layout follow what changes together and when (C2). |
| T9 | Tree or overlapping structure | Simon's hierarchy; Alexander 1964 | Alexander 1965 | Closed systems at several scales and from several views, related by elaboration, not one tree (C1). |
| T10 | Theory or documentation | Naur: the theory cannot be recovered from artifacts | Design records, decision logs, patterns | The design explains itself constructively; the record of why lives beside it, read-only (D5). The Skill accepts Naur's limit, which is why it gives questions and cases rather than rules alone. |
| T11 | Correct design or fitness | "The right thing": consistency, completeness | Alexander's fit; Gabriel's and Gall's survival | Effectiveness over completeness (A2); completeness is not the stopping criterion (the closure drive). |
| T12 | Cleanliness or evolution under pressure | Strategic investment in design; the health of the code base | Throwaway code that survives; worse is better | Revision is routine and done at premises, in large coherent steps, with removals made inside the work that builds the owner rather than as cleanup sweeps (D4, C4). |

**Gall's law against a concrete object early.** The literature says a working complex system grows from a working simple one; the source history's owner forbade pilots, toy realizations and minimal end-to-end cases as the route to an architecture ([cases.md](cases.md), E4, E14). The resolution is about what the simple working thing is. What must exist early is a concrete object that can be argued with: processes, tables, messages and flows, tied to the real world it runs in, then a product built in coherent transitions toward its final form (D1, C4). A demonstration that runs is not that object, and a pilot grown into an architecture brings the pilot's shape with it. Gall is right that a design with no footing fails; the footing asked for is the world and the object, not a toy.

**Essential against accidental.** Brooks and Moseley and Marks disagree about how much complexity is essential, and neither gives a procedure, because the label depends on what is held fixed as "the problem". The methodology supplies one by three questions asked together: is this something the system is answerable for (J1 The responsibility question); does it cross a real boundary (J2 The boundary test); does some party act differently on it (J5 The consumer test). What passes all three is essential; the rest is accidental, however elegant. This is offered as a contribution, not a restatement.

## Where the methodology restates, sharpens or departs

Each reading's treatment keeps its form: for the principles and judgment moves, by kind of claim; for the move groups F, R, M and L, what the sources say and then where the methodology stands, with one further kind, extends, where a move answers a case a source sets aside.

### The principles and the judgment moves

The first reading mapped its sources against the seven principles of the source history's last design, having read only those principles, not the rounds behind them; that mapping was inference. What follows extends it to the methodology as a whole, with claims of novelty limited to what the sources read can support.

#### Restates

- **Closure and hierarchy.** C1 restates Simon's near-decomposable hierarchy and adds Alexander's later point that designed things are not one tree.
- **Boundaries by what they hide.** C2 restates Parnas 1972: a boundary is justified by the decision it protects and by what changes, not by a name or a processing step.
- **Function placement.** B1's "a system owns exactly the knowledge only it has" restates the end-to-end argument; its "let every other fact be carried by its owner" restates the exokernel's split of protection from management.
- **Separation of concerns.** B2 restates Dijkstra and applies him: a concern separated in thought earns no runtime of its own until a real boundary requires it.
- **Mechanism, not policy; defaults.** A3's "freedom is the default" and the source history's "defaults, not mandates" restate long practice (Lampson, X, convention over configuration, the last [U]). Nothing distinctive here.
- **Removal.** J9 The carrier rule is Chesterton's fence made operational.
- **Revision as normal.** D4 shares the premise of Lehman, Fowler's sacrificial architecture and Foote and Yoder's Reconstruction.
- **Safety and liveness.** B4's split restates the distinction from concurrency theory (Lamport, 1977; not in the surveyed list, [U]).

#### Sharpens

- **The consumer test.** C3 and J5 give information hiding an operational form for vocabularies: an outcome, reason, state, check or extension point exists as a separate case only where some party acts differently on it. The sources say modules should hide decisions; none gives this test for distinctions. In the source history it cut five outcome classes and about thirty reasons to three outcomes and text ([cases.md](cases.md), the mechanism case Outcomes).
- **A budget of rigour.** B4 applies safety and liveness as an allocation: the strict, every-interleaving form only for the few claims that corrupt state silently if wrong (reuse, absence of effect, readiness, collective order, in the source history's case), and ordinary engineering everywhere else. No source read gives this rule; it is the least anticipated by the surveyed literature.
- **The trust boundary against the robustness principle.** B4 places checks where independent producers meet and none inside the set of parts changed together, the same line the kernel draws between internal and user-facing interfaces. Inside that set a disagreement fails loudly instead of being tolerated, which is the opposite of Postel and agrees with RFC 9413's critique. In the source history the set was one release: contextual, resting on an objective-bound assumption (one team builds every process; a trusted single-tenant site).
- **One loss path.** B5 is "define errors out of existence" made structural, applied to whole components rather than to a function's semantics, with the corollary that no second defence is built for a failure the loss path covers and the caveat that the path must converge (what overflowed must not overflow again on restart). Crash-only design is the nearest relative (not in the surveyed list, [U]).
- **Representations as obligations.** B3 and J11 turn Moseley and Marks's accidental state into a test applied fact by fact: every independent representation of one fact is a consistency obligation by construction, and a check that copies agree is the symptom.
- **Concepts and objects.** B2 sharpens Daniel Jackson's purpose over name: what earns a concept runtime identity is a boundary it must cross, not its purpose alone; a purposeful concept with no boundary stays in the design language.
- **Essential and accidental.** The procedure above, under the tensions.

#### Departs

- **Gall and pilots.** The methodology rejects the pilot as the working simple system and puts the concrete object and its world in its place (above, under the tensions).
- **YAGNI read as evolution.** It agrees with YAGNI for special mechanisms and with Baldwin and Clark for foundations, but departs from the evolutionary reading of YAGNI and worse-is-better in asking for large coherent transitions toward the final form, with no minimal versions or placeholders (C4).
- **Postel.** As above.
- **A method of revision.** The literature treats revision as a primary activity (Lehman, the second-system effect, Gall, Fowler, Foote and Yoder) but its principles do not say how to revise. The methodology does: ask whether the constraint should be a requirement at all before trimming mechanisms (D4, J12); state for each removal what behaviour must hold and what carries it (J9); watch for regrowth under new names; run a counter-pass by someone who did not do the removing; treat the list removals are tested against as itself open to question. This is the methodology's largest addition to the sources surveyed.
- **One mind among agents.** Brooks's conceptual integrity assumes a few human minds; Conway and Naur assume human teams. The methodology keeps integrity across many agents through one spine, one cross-reading and escalation by semantic consequence (D6), and adds a failure the sources do not discuss: agents optimize for whatever is checked, so a check layer becomes a proxy goal (D3, the failure pattern *the check layer*). Whether Conway's and Naur's arguments transfer to agents without persistent memory was not addressed in what was read, and stays open.

### F · What a computational problem is

**Well posed is a relation between a problem and a solver.** Simon lists criteria for a well-structured problem and says "the criteria are not absolute, but generally express a relation between characteristics of a problem domain, on the one hand, and the characteristics and power of an implicit or explicit problem solving mechanism, on the other" [V]. Amarel measures difficulty by the space searched under a representation and a method: "A good measure of the difficulty of the problem task is given by the size of the N-state space that must be searched to find a solution" [V]. Williams, on reformulating integer programs: "Such reformulations must often be considered in conjunction with the solution strategy to be employed" [V]. Hamming ties importance to the solver too: "It's not the consequence that makes a problem important, it is that you have a reasonable attack" [E]. One situation has no single difficulty.

**Four ingredients.** Read together, Simon's criteria, Pólya's anatomy of a problem (unknown, data, condition, with the check whether the condition is "sufficient to determine the unknown") [V], the computability definition ("a problem is one that asks for a solution in terms of an algorithm") [E] and Jackson give a criterion for an acceptable answer, a representation in which the situation and the permitted moves can be written, a practicable amount of computation, and a link from the representation to the world the answer serves. The fourth is Simon's fifth criterion (the operators "reflect with complete accuracy ... the laws (laws of nature) that govern the external world") and Jackson's domain properties; the formal tradition omits it by taking the instance as the whole story.

**The problem is in the world.** Jackson: "The problem is located in the world; the computer, executing our program text, is the solution" [V]. A requirement (what is wanted of the world), domain properties (what is true of it whatever the machine does) and a machine specification are kept apart, and the machine with the domain properties must entail the requirement. Jackson sets aside problems that "do not partake of the physical nature of the world", which on the reader's reading includes a runtime or a language. Brooks places the difficulty in the problem's statement: "The hardest single part of building a software system is deciding precisely what to build" [V]; his split of essence and accident is in his entry above. Marr's three levels and Newell and Simon's distinction between the task environment and the problem space [S] give the problem a level of its own and leave open where a problem space comes from.

**Where the methodology stands.**

- *Restates.* F1's four parts are the literature's ingredients. F3 takes Jackson's cut as its first test for problems located in a world: an element belongs to a solution's assumptions if it can change without changing the requirement or the domain properties.
- *Sharpens.* Amarel and Williams show formulation as a lever on cost; F1 turns the lever into a criterion: a formulation is two lists, what it fixes and what it leaves free, and a restriction earns its place by what the solver derives from it. The three homes of a matter (fixed by the formulation; bound when the environment is known; decided during operation) and the two guards (nothing of a solution's facts flows up into the fixed meaning; nothing of the fixed meaning flows down to remove a solver's meaningful choice) have no counterpart in the sources. The source history shows the guards are independent: a closure pass that satisfied the first violated the second ([cases.md](cases.md), P12, P14).
- *Extends.* F4 answers the case Jackson sets aside. When the problem's world is itself computational, the world is the software stacks that exist and the domain properties are their documented semantics; the source history's owner said so on 2026-09-25 ([cases.md](cases.md), P10). F2 restates the objective, for a system whose choices are made by strategies meant to improve, as properties of the space in which they choose, which no source treats; that form is objective-bound (I⁵ Round 01; [cases.md](cases.md), P18).
- *Departs.* F5 and F6 rest on no source. The sources treat a problem space as the solver's representation and ask how to search it; none says that a problem answered against concepts is not yet formulated, that tests run inside a register cannot test the register, or that a closure over a problem set certifies relations among answers and not the set's ontology. Alexander's 1965 remark that "In a single mental act you can only visualize a tree" [V] is the nearest: the structure in the representation may be the representer's. F7's ground is C4's (YAGNI and Fowler, in the sources above).

### R · Recursive expansion and the reuse of mechanisms

**Three things called "the recursion".** Kowalski: "The logic component determines the meaning of the algorithm whereas the control component only affects its efficiency", and of one definition of factorial, "Bottom-up use of the definition behaves like iteration. Top-down use behaves like recursive evaluation" [V]. The recursion is a property of how a definition is used. Reading Kowalski with Cilk, Cascades and Skyframe separates a recursion in the definition of a problem, in a method of solving, and in a runtime; the errors the sources warn of report a property of the second or third as a property of the first.

**Few kinds, one combination.** Simon: "Hierarchic systems are usually composed of only a few different kinds of subsystems" [V]. SICP's closure property ("the results of combining things with that operation can themselves be combined using the same operation") is the condition for hierarchies of any depth [V]. Hughes: "Our ability to decompose a problem into parts depends directly on our ability to glue solutions together" [V]. Jackson decomposes into sub-problems of known, different kinds, and "understanding a composite frame means a lot more than understanding its component subproblems" [V]. Karp reuses one solver across problem kinds by reduction, which is reuse and not recursion [V]. Dijkstra's THE system is a strict hierarchy of different abstractions, not self-similar [V], and Parnas holds that "hierarchical structure and 'clean' decomposition are two desirable but independent properties of a system structure" [V] (his decomposition criterion is in his entry above).

**Identity and sharing.** Five systems state the same conditions in their own vocabularies. Cascades makes a sub-problem's identity the expression with its cost limit and required physical properties, and "Reusing plans derived earlier is the crucial aspect of dynamic programming and memorization" [V]. The build-systems paper defines correctness by the name: "if we recompute the value of the key (using the task description, and the final store), we should get exactly the same value as we see in the final store" [V]. Skyframe forbids reads outside requested dependencies, since such a read "is an unregistered dependency and as such, can cause incorrect incremental builds"; Salsa "assumes that `your_program` is a purely deterministic function of its inputs, or else this whole setup makes no sense" [V]. MAXQ shares a sub-task through a parameterised name and narrows what the key must carry [V]; SICP shows memoization saving work only "sometimes" [V]. The conditions: a name derived from the problem alone; purity of the answer in that name and its named dependencies; a record of what the answer depended on. The shared failure is an input the name omits. Identity can also be discovered late: Cascades merges groups found equal, and Shake allows "further dependencies to be specified after examining the results of previous dependencies" [V], so the memo itself needs a repair path.

**Recursion in the runtime.** Cilk turns a spawn into a closure that is "ready if it has obtained all of its arguments" and steals work from the shallowest ready thread; its bounds hold for "fully strict" computations [V]. Cascades keeps pending work as task objects on a stack; Skyframe restarts a function whose dependency is not ready; MLIR reuses one container ("operations contain a list of regions, regions contain a list of blocks") at every level [V]. Where sources build one entity per unit, the reason is not the recursion: Armstrong maps processes to concurrent activities of the world, with a grain ("one process per user and not one process for every atom in the user's body") [V]; Erman and colleagues warn that too fine a grain "can decrease, rather than increase, the performance of the system" [V]; THE's levels serve resource management and testing [V]. No source read argues for a process or an owner per level of a recursive decomposition on the strength of the recursion alone.

**The stopping case and lifting.** Simon: "it is somewhat arbitrary as to where we leave off the partitioning" [V]. Wirth: refinement "terminates when all instructions are expressed in terms of an underlying computer or programming language" [V]. MLIR keeps higher structure as long as it is useful, since "Attempts to raise semantics once lowered are fragile" [V]. On lifting a solved sub-structure into a primitive, the sources say what it does and not when it pays. Sutton, Precup and Singh: "there is no guarantee that multi-step options will reduce the overall expense of planning", an opaque option can be slower than an interrupted one, and a "utility problem" arises "when too many macros are used" [V]. Dietterich: "there might exist better hierarchical policies where the policy for a subtask must be locally suboptimal so that the overall policy is optimal" [V]. Erol, Hendler and Nau: decomposition methods add expressive power, and "HTN planning is undecidable under even a very severe set of constraints" [V]. Amarel: macro-transitions "act as well-chosen lemmas", found only in a representation that shows "the critical 'narrows'" of the space [V]. Soar's chunking "acquires rules from goal-based experience" (abstract) [V]; its costs are [S].

**Choice and derivation.** Kowalski admits that "The distinction between logic and control is not wholly unambiguous" and prefers the analysis that puts efficiency in control so the logic stays a clear statement of the problem [V]. Reynolds shows a meta-circular interpreter inheriting the host's order of evaluation into the meaning of the language it defines [V]. The build-systems paper separates a scheduler from a rebuilder as independent choices [V].

**Coupling.** Simon's near-decomposability holds within a time window, with aggregate coupling beyond it [V]. Wirth's eight queens are coupled through earlier placements and need backtracking [V]. Amarel finds a decomposing formal system no help where rules are "strongly context dependent" [V]. Alexander's overlapping units are what a tree cannot name [V]. Nilsson's AND/OR graphs assume independent branches [S]; operations research coordinates coupled sub-problems by Dantzig-Wolfe and Benders methods [U].

**Where the methodology stands.**

- *Restates.* R2's three conditions for sharing are the literature's. R3's form, recursion carried as data and a small procedure, is the regularity above. R5's "the stopping case is chosen, not found" is Simon's, Wirth's and MLIR's. R7's conditions are near-decomposability and independence under four descriptions. R1's "decomposition and composition are designed together" is Hughes's and Jackson's.
- *Sharpens.* R2 adds two tests of identity, content (only what changes the obligation, never scheduling history) and computability (anyone can derive it from the fixed meaning), and a line the sources do not draw: the remainder of a sub-problem is a function of its identity and the facts, never part of its identity. The source history needed a whole mechanism of successor goals because it drew that line wrongly ([cases.md](cases.md), P15, P16). R2 also orders the three kinds of sharing (work in flight, results, reusable structures): use what exists, else wait on what is coming, else propose. R3 states the regularity's reason and its condition: derived, not stored, holds where the facts the derivation needs can be read back. In the source history the fixed meaning was immutable, acyclic and region-exact; where dependencies are discovered by solving, Shake and Skyframe record them and derive over the record, which meets the same condition another way. R4 names a recursion by what it may decide and where it ends, allows a coupling between two recursions only as a lowering at a named point, and treats recursion, hierarchy and decomposition as independent properties (Parnas with Kowalski). R7 adds joint execution for parts that exchange as they run, and calls a global feasibility proof over the tree a manufactured requirement.
- *Departs.* The first framing of the problem wanted an open instruction vocabulary at run time, with stable subtrees lifted into new instructions by the live solver. The source history placed lifting outside the live solving, as a new candidate the procedure chooses among, because its fixed Program had to hold for a run; R5 states this as a condition, and elsewhere lifting during the solve is a design choice with its record. The literature names the ways lifting loses and is silent on where it is done; Soar's chunking lifts during problem solving. And where MLIR lowers progressively through representations inside a compiler, the history refused a lowering tower as the structure of the running system: the agreement is that structure is kept high while it is useful, the departure is in where levels are allowed to become runtime things.

### M · Reformulation

**Three forms.** The sources use one word for three operations. *Re-rendering*: Amarel's coarser elements and macro-steps, Williams's sharp formulations, whose LP relaxation "gives the convex hull of IP solutions" [V]. *Replacement*: Pólya's auxiliary problem, "a problem which we consider, not for its own sake, but because we hope that its consideration may help us to solve another problem", with generalization, specialization and the inventor's paradox, that the more ambitious problem "may be more accessible" [V]. *Change of what is asked*: Rittel and Webber's level, Schön's problem setting, "by which we define the decision to be made, the ends to be achieved, and the means that may be chosen" [E], Lakatos's barring of monsters and exceptions and incorporation of lemmas, which change a concept, a claim's domain or its conditions [V] (headings only for the later methods).

**The level.** Rittel and Webber: "There is nothing like a natural level of a wicked problem. Of course, the higher the level of a problem's formulation, the broader and more general it becomes: and the more difficult it becomes to do something about it. On the other hand, one should not try to cure symptoms"; and attacking "on too low a level" may result "in making things worse" [V]. They restrict the claim to wicked problems, and set equation-solving aside as tame.

**Losing the original.** Pólya: "we may stray so far from our original problem that we are in danger of losing it altogether. Yet there is a good question that may bring us back to it: Did you use all the data? Did you use the whole condition?" [V]. Lakatos's monster-barring saves a claim by redefining its concept [V]. Brooks: "descriptions of a software entity that abstract away its complexity often abstract away its essence" [V].

**What the sources do not show.** Their reformulations are retrospective. Amarel's five formulations, Pólya's worked problems and Williams's models are presented after the fact as paths from worse to better, with no dead ends and no team in the middle of a difficulty choosing between diagnoses. Restructuring in insight and Kuhn's anomalies were not verified [U, S].

**Where the methodology stands.**

- *Restates.* M2's three forms are the literature's, made explicit, each with its test: equivalence of what is asked; transfer of the answer back; acceptance by those who rely on it. M2's choice of level is Rittel and Webber's, with the cost on each side stated. M4's check of the new formulation against what the original demanded is Pólya's question.
- *Sharpens.* M1 reads a difficulty three ways (capability, design, formulation) and treats them, with Simon, Amarel and Williams, as three places to intervene rather than three causes; it adds what the sources do not: where the formulation manufactured the difficulty, intervening there is cheapest, because the mechanisms that served it fall away together. M3's signs (a tension rediscovered under several vocabularies; a ranking contradicted within a day; a question whose premise rides in its options; a word naming two problems) are diagnostic where the sources give remedies. M4 asks what a removed representation silently supplied and requires a replacement to pass the same test; it records which form each reformulation takes, for each party that relies on the answer. "Move to another owner" needs a receiving owner with the question in a usable form, which Jackson's inside-out method (a difficulty recast as a sub-problem of another frame) assumes without saying.
- *Departs.* The Skill presents reformulation as moves with signs and a record, never as a sequence, because the history's reformulations were made with dead ends that a retrospective path hides.

### L · The two loops

**Co-evolution.** Brooks: "it is really impossible for clients, even those working with software engineers, to specify completely, precisely, and correctly the exact requirements of a modern software product before having built and tried some versions of the product they are specifying" [V]. Rittel and Webber: "one cannot first understand, then solve" [V]. Dorst and Cross report from nine designers that problem and solution are developed and refined together [S]. Alexander's misfit, found only by making a form [S], is the same evidence from the side of design.

**Tempos.** Simon: "If the continuing alteration of the problem representation is short-term and reversible, we generally call it 'adaptation' or 'feedback'; if the alteration is more or less permanent (e.g., revising the 'laws of nature'), we refer to it as 'learning'" [V].

**Where the methodology stands.**

- *Restates.* That the formulation is revised throughout the work, and that neither loop completes before the other starts, is the literature's consensus.
- *Sharpens.* L1 names the loop and the tempo; the source history had three tempos (the solver revising its view at every event, knowledge revised between runs, the problem revised between rounds), one more than Simon names. L1 also gives a switch its form: name what the preceding mode has been doing and deny that it is the question. L3 names the carriers and counts what they carried (the silences below). L4's floor, written as the exceptions to the current reformulation with the failure each guards, has no counterpart in the sources.
- *Departs.* Brooks's remedy is building and trying versions; the history forbade a pilot or toy realization whose shape becomes the architecture and put a concrete object and research against a converged formulation in its place, while a probe that measures the world stays part of forming the problem (F5) (L2, and the discussion of Gall's law under the tensions). Where Rittel and Webber find no stopping rule, the history found that a limit is findable only against an explicit, converged formulation ([cases.md](cases.md), P18).

## The silences, and what the history supplies

The two readers of the second reading found four questions the verified literature leaves open. For each, what the source history supplies is stated with the strength of its evidence: it is one system, and most findings rest on one episode or on the readers' own codings.

**1. A procedure for the three-way diagnosis.** No verified source tells whether a difficulty is a missing capability, a wrong design or a formulation to change. Pólya has remedies and says no infallible method exists; Amarel diagnoses after the fact; Simon dissolves the question by placing revision of the problem space inside the solver; Rittel and Webber give symptoms for a class of problems; Jackson's entailment catches one kind of formulation fault, a requirement the machine's interface cannot reach; Williams's cost signal works only where "same problem" has a formal meaning. *What the history supplies:* the diagnosis practised and counted. Of 416 inherited problems re-asked of a concrete object, 29 did not arise in it, 41 were answered by an existing supplier or belonged to another owner, and 334 were real in the object ([cases.md](cases.md), P11). Of 25 construction questions, 15 rested on a premise of their own representation, 4 were a missing capability, 2 a design fault and 4 the owner's choice; of 11 later questions, 7, 2, 1 and 1 (P17). And a diagnostic for the third reading: if a stronger capability would not change the judgment, the difficulty is not one of capability. *Strength:* one system; the fates are agents' classifications against one object, and the share that did not arise measures the owner's premises (nine of the 29 rest on one decider per run); the question codings are one reader's.

**2. The relation between a problem's representation and the runtime structure that solves it.** The theory sources hold one solver with a representation as its data; Amarel's separation of a translator from a solver is the only remark on program structure; Jackson's frames decompose the problem and leave the machine to the developer; Parnas says the module structure that follows the processing steps is the habitual one and not the better one. The recursion literature shows a regularity (recursion carried as data, entities justified by something else) without stating it as a relation. *What the history supplies:* "derived, not stored", with four concrete forms (decision recursion as heap data on one thread; sub-problem identity as a key in a lookup; the construction of a computation flattened to one record; responsibility carried by the operating system's own hierarchy), reached after the problem's recursion had been given a worker tree, a resident cursor, sessions and a task per goal, each removed (P3 to P16). *Strength:* one system, under a stated condition: the facts the derivation needs can be read back (in the source history, from an immutable, acyclic, region-exact Program). The history has no problem whose dependencies are found only by solving; the build systems that have one, the case Shake describes, record those dependencies and derive over them, which meets the condition another way.

**3. A test for whether a decomposition is in the problem or in the solver.** The sources give partial tests from separate fields: Kowalski's equivalence of logic under different control, the build-systems correctness definition, Reynolds's test that the host's order must not leak, Dietterich's gap between local and global optima, Parnas's hiding criterion, Jackson's phenomena shared with the world. None combines them; Alexander, Kowalski and Reynolds agree the confusion is real. *What the history supplies:* two statements that pass the combined test, "Any term derives the same descriptions from the same Program" and "two implementations may pick differently" (P16), and two tests of its own for an element of a formulation: what would a second, independent implementation need to behave the same at the boundaries and after loss; and, built today from the real behaviours and the substrates available, would this structure still emerge (F3). *Strength:* one episode, the source history's Rounds 16 to 18; the tests detect leakage from solver into problem and say nothing about whether a cut is the useful one.

**4. A criterion for when lifting pays.** The sources say what a lifted primitive does (it may leave its formal class, hide a reason to interrupt it, add expressive power, settle on a local optimum, slow selection when there are too many) and give no measure for deciding to lift. *What the history supplies:* a procedure, not a criterion. A stable sub-structure was to be lifted outside the live solving, because the Program had to hold for a run, as a new candidate (a larger implementation, a new primitive, a new Program) that the same procedure then chooses among; a Kernel is atomic within a Program, and decomposing it is Intelligence's act, taking effect only in a new Program (P14, P16). *Strength:* a rule adopted, not a practice observed. The first framing's runtime lifting was never built, "No reviewed generation supported discovering a recurring subtree and re-encapsulating it" (agents' text, axle Round 02, RS-37), and the history records no lifting performed under the rule.

A fifth silence is narrower. No verified source treats recursive decomposition of decision authority or responsibility, as distinct from computation; Armstrong's supervision tree is justified by failure. The pre-project workspace's policies of declared scope, delegating responsibility, authority and knowledge together through handoffs (P5b), are the history's own material on it: objective-bound, from one workspace, built as a library and exercised in a three-policy example.
