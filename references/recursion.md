## How to read these moves

A sub-problem, a solver and a recursion are words for understanding a problem. They describe how a problem is expanded against a fixed meaning: meeting this obligation needs those, which need these, until something is answered directly. None of them names a thing the system must contain. A system that solves a recursively expanded problem may have no object called a problem, no hierarchy of solvers and no protocol between levels; in the most mature form the source history reached, it had none of these, and the expansion was a function evaluated when needed. Where this reference cites a runtime form, it says what real boundary justified it.

Three different things are called "the recursion", and they are kept apart throughout: a recursion in the definition of the problem (an obligation defined by smaller obligations of the same kind); a recursion in a method of solving (top-down reduction, refinement, divide and conquer); and a recursion in the runtime (nested calls, tasks, processes, supervisors). One definition can be solved by several methods, and one method carried by several runtime forms. Kowalski's one definition of factorial "behaves like iteration" used bottom-up and like "recursive evaluation" used top-down. Most mistakes about recursion report a property of the method or the runtime as one of the problem: the tree a method walked is taken for the problem's, or a decomposition's depth for a count of processes.

Recursion is a form, and a form has a boundary; the next section states it, and every move is read with it. Each move gives the question, the consequence, why, what it is not, when to reach for it, the signs of violating it, and a worked application outside the domain the moves were learned in; the applications are examples, not answers. Examples from the source history are marked with their kind (enduring, contextual or objective-bound) and told in full in [cases.md](cases.md). A quotation from that history replaces the project's name in brackets and is located as "(the owner of the source history, <date>; `cases.md` P<n>)" or "(agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P<n>)". The principles and judgment moves that make the same point for the system are linked, not restated.

## The running examples

- **A build system with dynamic dependencies.** It decides what must be rebuilt, runs actions locally or on remote executors, and caches outputs locally and in a shared cache. Some rules discover their dependencies while running: a compile learns which headers it read; a generator's output decides which further targets exist.
- **A query planner with a memo.** A cost-based optimizer that explores equivalent plans, groups logically equivalent expressions, and remembers the best plan for each group under the properties a consumer requires.
- **A proof search.** A prover that reduces a goal (hypotheses entail a conclusion) by tactics, each yielding subgoals. Goals may mention unknowns (metavariables) that another goal's proof will fix.
- **A rendering pipeline.** A real-time renderer with a scene graph, a sequence of passes, screen tiles processed on a GPU, materials compiled from graphs of shader nodes, and memory pools for frame buffers.
- **An incremental type checker.** The core of a language server: after each edit it answers queries (the signature of a declaration, the type of a body, the members of a module), reusing what the edit did not invalidate.

## When recursion is the form, and when it is not

Recursion is the right form when three things hold together. The sub-problems are of the same kind as the problem, or of a few kinds one procedure handles (R1). They are independent or nearly so: a part's answer can be computed without its siblings' answers, or with them only in aggregate and within a stated window (R7). And there is a place to stop, chosen on purpose, where the formulation stops defining structure or another party owns what lies below (R5). The literature states the independence condition in four vocabularies that agree (Simon's near-decomposability within a time window; Nilsson's independent AND branches, known here from secondary accounts; the "fully strict" computations for which Cilk's scheduler has its bounds; Wirth's partial solutions rejected early), and each source says what happens when it fails: backtracking, a different optimum, undecidability, a unit the tree cannot name.

Recursion is not the form, or only in part, in four situations, each with a sign.

Where the problem is a few large pieces rather than many self-similar ones, choice matters more than division. Example from the source history (objective-bound): a language designed so that array computations could be decomposed exactly by region met real programs that were mostly a few whole-array bodies, and the record's own doubt was that "Candidate selection and placement may matter far more than division for those programs"; only measurement could settle it. The sign: an expansion of one or two levels, with nearly all the effort in choosing among realizations of the top.

Where parts must run concurrently while exchanging data, they are one joint execution, not independent problems. Example (contextual): over a domain split into blocks, with dependencies reaching r cells per step, a run of many steps confined to one block is not convex, so a window of many steps cannot be split into per-block problems. The sign: a part that needs its neighbour's intermediate results, not its final one.

Where the objective does not separate, the best parts do not make the best whole. Dietterich states it for hierarchical learning: a "recursively optimal" policy, each sub-task optimal given its children, can be worse than the best hierarchical policy, which may need a sub-task to be locally suboptimal. The sign: some part must do worse on its own terms for the whole to do well.

Where a step is a one-shot effect on the world (charging a card), it is not expanded; it is a leaf by its nature (R7 states the neighbouring limits).

Between these lies the common case, a problem both recursive and coupled. Then recursion organizes the coupled problem and the coupling is carried as a fact at each sub-problem's boundary (R7). The owner of the source history put this as the limit of his own mechanism: "Recursion is a way to organize and solve a coupled problem, not an assumption that all subproblems are naturally independent" (the owner of the source history, September 2026; `cases.md` P4).

Recursion is never a requirement on a system: it is a property of how a problem is solved against its fixed meaning, and it earns nothing in the runtime by itself.

## R1 Same kind, same procedure

**Question.** Is this sub-problem of the same kind as its parent, and does one procedure answer every level without a new case?

**Consequence.** Where one procedure handles every level without a new case, mechanism or vocabulary per level, the decomposition is recursive there. What is uniform is the procedure and its protocol (what is owed, what counts as meeting it, what is legal, how a piece is handed on); the content differs by kind, and the kinds are few. Where a level needs its own case, either the kind is wrong or the level is an atomic case (R5). Decomposition and composition are designed together. An answer that is an estimate (a score, a label near a boundary, a verdict that varies between runs) is a fact with a source and an uncertainty that choices read, never a term of the fixed meaning; where the procedure can wait, a wait may name the evidence that would narrow it. The source history drew the same line where it met uncertain answers: an unknown outcome was a state of knowledge, not a failure (`cases.md` P9), and "a sample is not a reservation" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P16).

**Why.** A procedure written once applies at any depth, so a problem of unbounded size is handled by a bounded description; that works only if the parts' answers can be combined at every level by the same means. Hughes: "Our ability to decompose a problem into parts depends directly on our ability to glue solutions together."

Example from the source history (objective-bound): the mature solver's procedure gave one of three answers, a step, a wait naming the facts that could change the answer, or "no admissible choice", the last given only after every fact already received had been applied, so that silence never produced it. A procedure with those three answers and no fourth is a function of what is known, which is what R3 relied on there: it could run at any time, from any state, and nobody polled or blocked. A synchronous divide-and-conquer has no wait and needs none; R1 asks only that one procedure answer every level.

Between a single closed combination (one kind, one glue) and Jackson's catalogue of problem kinds, each with its own composition concerns, sits a middle form: one procedure over a few kinds. Example from the source history (enduring in form, objective-bound in content): the mature solver had six kinds of owed work (advance the committed frontier, deliver to a destination, obtain a value, form a cover, make a prepared computation ready, commit), each stating what is owed and when it is met, all answered by one procedure. Its composition rule was designed with them: the actions of one step went to their executors independently, and when one was refused after another took effect, both outcomes were applied and the next derivation found what remained ("Steps are not transactions"; agents' text of the source history, `cases.md` P16).

**What it is not.** Not recursion for the form's sake: "If a region can be completed directly, it need not be subdivided merely for the sake of preserving a recursive form" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P5a). Not a prerequisite of the system: a decision method may use dynamic programming or integer optimization without the system depending on either. Not uniformity of content: a generic layer forced over mechanisms that differ is the failure pattern uniformity for its own sake ([C2 Boundaries from responsibility, not symmetry](principles.md#c2-boundaries-from-responsibility-not-symmetry)).

**When to reach for it.** When a decomposition is drawn and someone says "and then the same thing happens at the next level". When a level is acquiring a special case. When a level waits for the one below to return.

**Signs you are violating it.** Each level has its own name for the same act. Kinds are added per situation until they outnumber the levels. The procedure needs to know its level. Pieces sit "pending" with no named source of change, or fail because nothing was heard. A part that could be answered directly is split to keep the tree regular.

**Worked application: a proof search.** A tactic reduces a goal to subgoals of the same kind, and one procedure (choose a tactic, apply it, take on its subgoals) answers every level. The kinds are few: introduce an implication, split a conjunction, case on a disjunction, induct, rewrite, call a decision procedure. Each states what it owes back, a proof built from its subgoals' proofs, and that is the composition: a case split whose cases do not cover the hypothesis cannot be glued into a proof, so it is not a legal step. The history's three answers translate directly here. A step is a tactic applied. A wait is a goal mentioning a metavariable another goal will fix, and it names that metavariable. "No admissible choice" is a goal to which no tactic applies and in which no open unknown could change that; it fails the goal, and the failure propagates to whatever depended on it. A goal in linear arithmetic is closed by a decision procedure in one step; splitting it into cases to keep the recursion uniform would be recursion for its own sake. The decision procedure is an atomic case (R5).

## R2 Identity by description

**Question.** Can any party derive the same name for this sub-problem from the same fixed meaning and facts, and does the name hold exactly what changes the obligation and nothing of how it is being solved?

**Consequence.** Name a sub-problem by a canonical description in the problem's terms. Sharing then follows from identity rather than from a mechanism: equal descriptions are one sub-problem, work in flight serves all its consumers, and where sub-problems are shared the structure of demand is a graph, not a tree. The name passes two tests. Content: it holds only what changes the obligation, never scheduling history and never what remains to be done. Computability: anyone can compute it from the fixed meaning. What remains of a sub-problem is a function of its identity and the facts. Where the fixed meaning is versioned while sub-problems are open (a contract amended, a model rebuilt), the version in force is part of the description (see the condition under [R3](#r3-derived-not-stored)). Identity can be shared while each party keeps its own representation of the content. Where identity is discovered during solving, the memo needs a repair path. The conditions for correct sharing are in [Sharing](#sharing-the-three-conditions-and-the-failure-that-breaks-them).

**Why.** An allocated name, a handle minted when a sub-problem was formed, can be resolved only through whoever minted it; across parties it needs a registry, where a derived name needs none. Within one owner an allocated name can still share, through that owner's lookup (a query optimizer's groups carry allocated identities and share through its memo); across parties, two that form the same sub-problem independently get two names and do the work twice, while a derived name lets the second compute the same name and find the first. Example from the source history (enduring; [cases.md](cases.md), P16): goals were first identified by an integer allocated inside one process, so only that process could name a goal; later, owed work was "named by its description in Program terms", and "Any term derives the same descriptions from the same Program" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P16), so advisers, producers and a successor process could refer to the same work, and sharing needed no registry.

The content test catches a subtler error. If what remains is part of the name, every partial completion changes the name, and the old sub-problem must be migrated and its consumers moved. In the same history a residual successor, a migration of interests and an empty attempt record per successor all came from that one misplaced line: "The design needed migration only because it put the unsatisfied remainder *inside* the key" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P16). Moving the line removed the mechanism.

**What it is not.** Not identity by allocation. Not identity by position (a path from a root), which differs for the same sub-problem reached two ways. Not a cure for overlap: demands that overlap without being equal are different sub-problems, and the choice of atom or an exact cover handles them, not sharing.

**When to reach for it.** When a sub-problem is about to receive an identifier, or work is done twice.

**Signs you are violating it.** A registry of sub-problems kept consistent with the work. A key holding a counter, a timestamp, an attempt number or a scheduler's handle. Migration code for partly completed work. Two consumers unable to tell they need the same thing. Reuse silently wrong after an input changed.

**Worked application: a query planner's memo.** The name of a sub-problem is a group of equivalent expressions together with what the consumer requires; Graefe's optimization goal "combines a group or expression with a cost limit and with required and excluded physical properties". Apply the content test. Required sort order changes the obligation: the best plan for a join whose output must be sorted is not the best unsorted plan, and a memo keyed by the group alone returns the wrong plan to a consumer that needed order. The cost limit is of another nature: it bounds the search, closer to how the sub-problem is solved than to what it is, and keeping it in the name stops searches with different budgets from sharing. A planner may keep it there deliberately; the move asks that it know which kind of field it is keeping. Apply the computability test: any rule, given the query and the required properties, computes the same name, so when a transformation produces an expression already in another group, the planner learns that two groups are one and merges them. The merge is the repair path discovered identity needs.

## R3 Derived, not stored

**Question.** Is the expanded structure a derivation recomputable from what is durable and what can be observed, or an object hierarchy the runtime must keep consistent?

**Consequence.** Derive it when it is needed. Store only what crosses a real boundary: durable progress, work in flight, choices adopted and not yet carried out, facts only a remote party knows. The solver can then be one procedure re-run on each event, a restartable function whose result may be "wait". Where that form is chosen, nobody waits for a lower level to return, and entering, taking over after a failure and starting fresh become one path. Where an entity per unit is built (a process, a task, an owner), it is justified by something the recursion does not supply (concurrency in the world, failure containment, a distinct source of knowledge, resource management), and its grain is bounded by its cost.

**Why.** A stored expansion is a second copy of what the fixed meaning and the facts already determine, and each copy brings a consistency obligation: every event must update it, every failure reconcile it, every successor rebuild it. [B2 A concept is not an object](principles.md#b2-a-concept-is-not-an-object) and [J2 The boundary test](judgment.md#j2-the-boundary-test) hold this in general; R3 is its form for a problem's recursion, the concept most easily mistaken for an object because its shape is so clear.

Suspension is how the stored form usually returns. A solver written as nested calls, or a task per sub-problem, keeps each level's state on a stack or in a closure; if that state may not carry meaning, because the durable facts are the record, it must be re-read after every wait and is cost without benefit. Example from the source history (enduring): a task per goal was replaced by a function from the program, the facts and a ledger of work in flight to one of three answers; "Waiting is the function's answer, not a registration owned by a task" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P16). The literature shows the same regularity: Cilk, Cascades, Skyframe and MLIR carry recursion as data (closures, task objects, restartable functions, nested regions) moved by a small scheduler, and sources that build an entity per unit justify it by concurrency in the world, failure containment, distinct knowledge or testability, bounding its grain: "one process per user and not one process for every atom in the user's body" (Armstrong). None argues for a process or owner per level on the strength of the recursion alone.

**The condition.** Derivation from facts works when the facts the derivation needs can be read back. In the source history the fixed meaning was an immutable, acyclic program with exact regions, so the need relation could be re-evaluated at every event. Where dependencies are discovered by running a step, they are facts only the run revealed, so they are recorded, and the derivation runs over the record; the build-systems literature does this, and it is the same rule (store what only one party knows) applied to another kind of fact. The history has no instance of it. Where the fixed meaning is versioned while sub-problems are open (a contract amended while claims are handled, a model rebuilt in the middle of a campaign), the version in force is part of each sub-problem's description (R2). Example (objective-bound): the history's own answer was stricter, since a changed computation was a new program for a new run, so no open sub-problem ever met a second version.

**What it is not.** Not a tree of workers, a process per level, a registry of sub-problems, capacity delegated down a tree, a global feasibility proof over it, or suspended computations forbidden to carry meaning. Not a ban on state: what crosses a real boundary is stored, once, by its owner. Not a claim that re-derivation is free; making it cheap is an implementation freedom, and an index kept as a convenience, with no meaning of its own, is ordinary engineering.

**When to reach for it.** When a process, task or actor is proposed per sub-problem, or recovery must rebuild a structure that mirrors the problem.

**Signs you are violating it.** A structure kept consistent with the facts, with its own repair code. Recovery that differs from starting. A lifecycle per level. Cycle detection on insertion into a demand graph, where a derived form treats a cycle met while computing as an error of the fixed meaning. Cost per turn growing faster than the work; in the source history a persistent controller's cost per turn grew super-linearly above about 800 units, mostly in copying its own state.

**Worked application: a build system with dynamic dependencies.** A first design gives each target a node object in a tree mirroring the rule files, with a state machine, a thread waiting on its children, a registry, and a recovery step that rebuilds the tree after a crash. Ask what crosses a real boundary. Cached outputs, keyed by a digest of the action and its inputs, cross time and machines. Actions sent to remote executors and not yet answered are work in flight across a process boundary. The record of which inputs each action read is a fact only running it revealed. The rest is derivable: whether a target is up to date follows from its rule, the recorded reads and the current files. The build becomes one procedure run as events arrive: for the requested targets, derive what is needed, use what the cache has, wait on what is in flight, propose what remains. A rule that asks for a dependency not yet built answers "wait on that key" and is run again when the key is ready. After a crash the same procedure runs over the cache and the recorded reads. The per-target threads go; parallelism is sized by the executor pool, not by the depth of the rule files. Two cautions: a restarting rule may repeat work done before its dependency was ready (the build-systems literature finds restarting schedulers not minimal), which is a cost to weigh, not a reason to restore the tree; and every read must be recorded, since a missed read makes reuse silently wrong.

## R4 Which recursion

**Question.** Which of the things that recurse here is this: the problem's expansion, the construction of a computation, the mechanical composition of an act already decided, the nesting of native systems, the ownership of resources, the unfolding of a description, the layout of a repository?

**Consequence.** Name each recursion by what it may decide and where it ends, not by its shape. Keep the recursions distinct, with no structural isomorphism: the depth of one does not set the depth of another, and a level of the problem is not a node, a process, a rank, a pool or an authority. Where two must touch, the coupling is a lowering at a named point, never a correspondence of levels. Whether a recursion is a tree or a graph follows from the relation it carries: ownership and containment give trees; demand gives a graph with shared nodes.

**Why.** Recursive structures look alike and invite merging, and fused structures inherit each other's constraints. A decision structure that is also the ownership tree cannot share a sub-problem between owners; a process tree that mirrors the decomposition makes a problem's depth a count of processes. Example from the source history (enduring; [cases.md](cases.md), P10 and P15): the owner named the recursions of one system (the solver's decisions, the construction of a computation, the delegation of execution to workers) and wrote: "No structural isomorphism should be established among these three forms of recursion"; they "merely share a general structure of progressively narrowing something until it reaches a finite concrete entity" (the owner of the source history, 2026-09-26; `cases.md` P10). In the same history, "recursion is not runtime structure" was stated early, violated twice through a neighbouring concern (a controller per member of a parallel job; sessions grown over a node service), and restated; the owner's reading of what was built, not the principle, removed each violation.

Recursion (self-similar parts), hierarchy (a partial order of use) and decomposition (boundaries drawn by what is hidden) are independent properties. A layered operating system is hierarchical without recursion; a build graph shares nodes and is neither a tree nor a hierarchy of kinds.

**What it is not.** Not a tower of representations, each lowered into the next, and not a stack of runtimes forwarding work level by level. Not a ban on coupling: a named lowering point is how two recursions properly meet.

**When to reach for it.** When someone says "the levels correspond", or diagrams of the decomposition and of processes or ownership have the same shape.

**Signs you are violating it.** The number of processes depends on the depth of the problem. A sub-problem cannot be shared because it belongs to one subtree. Capacity is delegated down the decomposition. One word names two recursions (a "worker" that is a unit of decision and of execution). Ownership of a resource follows the shape of the decision that requested it.

**Worked application: a rendering pipeline.** Several things recurse. The scene graph nests transforms and objects (a recursion in the description). Rendering is split into passes and screen tiles (a recursion in the method). Materials are composed from shader nodes (the construction of a computation). The driver nests its command and context hierarchies (a native recursion). Memory pools are sub-allocated to frames and passes (ownership). A first design maps them onto one another, a render task per scene-graph node owning its buffers and spawning tasks for its children. But the scene graph says how transforms compose, not how work should be divided, which depends on screen coverage and cost; tasks bound to nodes make a deep, thin hierarchy expensive and a wide one unbalanced. Name each by what it decides and where it ends: the scene graph decides world transforms and ends in a flat list of draw items; tiling decides which pixels a unit of work covers and ends in a dispatch; the shader graph decides what a material computes and ends in one compiled program with no record per node. They touch at named points: draw items are binned into tiles in one place, a program is bound to a draw item in one place, a pass takes buffers from the frame's pool in one place. The demand among passes is a graph: one light's shadow map, needed by several passes, is computed once because its name (light, cascade, resolution) is equal for all of them (R2).

## R5 The atomic case

**Question.** Where does the expansion stop, by whose decision, and what is on the other side?

**Consequence.** The stopping case is chosen, not found. It sits where the fixed meaning stops defining structure or another party owns the next level, and the recursion may continue inside that party without the fixed meaning seeing it. An atomic case is opaque by decision, which differs from not having looked yet. Moving it is a change of representation: a new candidate (an implementation covering a larger piece, a new primitive, a new version of the fixed meaning), admitted to the candidates and chosen among by the same procedure. Where the solver's control or the fixed meaning must stay unchanged for the duration of a run, the move is made outside the live solving, in the slow loop; otherwise moving it during the solve is a design choice, made with its record and with the version of the meaning each answer used. How such candidates are found the history does not show; the literature's account (Amarel) is that they are seen by observing executions in a representation that shows where they narrow. Lifting and its bets are in [The atomic case and lifting](#the-atomic-case-and-lifting).

**Why.** Simon calls the choice of elementary components "somewhat arbitrary"; Wirth stops at the target language, SICP at the evaluator's primitives, MLIR at the dialect matching the execution model. A fixed stopping case serves a fixed purpose, a strength while the purpose holds. The stopping case also fixes what can be shared, since names are formed from atoms (R2). Example from the source history (objective-bound): the language made its user-written operation indivisible, so demand for any part of its output was demand for the whole instance, and overlapping requests shared one instance; inside its implementation the work could be tiled or distributed unseen; a computation that split the operation was "a different Program" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P14). Each stopping place in that solver had one of two reasons: "either the language says it is one operation ..., or a different party owns the next level" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P16).

Opacity has several senses: outside the formulation's grammar; untracked by choice; source unreadable; unverifiable even with access. The source history recorded the cost of conflating them: "'Not yet derived' was conflated with 'genuinely opaque'" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P3). A boundary placed because nobody had looked is placed by ignorance, not decision.

**What it is not.** Not, where the meaning must hold for a run, a vocabulary the solver changes during it; where it need not, a solver that learns lemmas or compiles traces as it runs has made a design choice and records it. Not a limit of legality: a formulation can be legal although nothing today computes its atoms. Not a permanent property of the problem: the atom moves when observation shows it misplaced, by producing a new representation.

**When to reach for it.** When a decomposition has no stated bottom, a solver is about to open a unit it was handed whole, or someone proposes that the solver learn primitives while it runs, which asks whether the meaning must hold for a run.

**Signs you are violating it.** The expansion goes down until patience runs out. A unit is called opaque, yet the solver reaches into it for special cases. A kind of piece is atomic in one place and expanded in another with no stated reason. The primitives change during a solve and nothing records which version of the fixed meaning an answer used.

**Worked application: an incremental type checker.** The designers choose the atom, what is recomputed as one unit when an edit invalidates it. With the file as atom, every keystroke re-checks the file; with every expression, the memo is enormous and mostly unused. A good choice is the declaration: each signature is one query and each function body another, the body opaque to signature queries by decision. That decision makes an edit inside a body cheap, since no other declaration's answer depends on it, and it holds while the language keeps bodies out of signatures. If a function's return type may be inferred from its body, an edit inside the body can change other declarations' answers, and the atom is reconsidered (a separate query for the inferred type), not patched with special invalidation. Lifting runs the other way: libraries re-checked from source at every start are given precomputed interface summaries, a new primitive standing for a solved sub-structure, produced by a build step outside the live checking and admitted as input. It is a bet that the library's internals will not be needed; a later feature that needs bodies finds the summary sealed, and the remedy is a new summary format, made outside the checker.

## R6 Fixed derivation, free choice (the control test)

**Question.** Which parts of the expansion are determined by the fixed meaning (what is owed, what counts as meeting it, which answers exist) and which are choices (how to split, which realization, how large a window)?

**Consequence.** The derivation is fixed and the same everywhere. A choice is a policy at a decision point. Where choices are supplied by parties outside the solver, a policy is improved outside the live solving and a proposal is checked against the facts current when it returns; the structure of choices (how many decision points) and their openness (how many are open to outside policies) are then sized separately, and a point is opened when there is a policy to put there. Where the sub-problems opened are chosen by the value of what they would reveal (search, sampling, triage), the expansion and its stopping are policies, and only what each sub-problem is follows from the fixed meaning; in the source history the Window and Split decision points were such policies (objective-bound). A fact belongs in the view a choice sees if it can change the legality, the expected cost or the future value of an available action. A domain's own "decision" (an adjudication, a ruling on a claim) is a derivation where the facts determine it and a choice only where they do not. The test, the control test: vary what is meant to be only control and see whether the answer changes.

**Why.** Kowalski split an algorithm into logic, which "determines the meaning", and control, which "only affects its efficiency", and admitted the split "is not wholly unambiguous", which is why a test is needed. His own equivalence supplies one: vary the order, top-down against bottom-up, sequential against parallel, one policy against another; if the answers change, the varied structure was in the problem or was wrongly called control. The build-systems definition of correctness is the same test: recomputing a key from the task description and the final store gives the stored value, whatever the schedule. Example from the source history (enduring): the mature design passed it in two sentences, "Any term derives the same descriptions from the same Program" and, of its default policies, "two implementations may pick differently" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P16). In the same history (objective-bound content), six decision points organized the solver's default policy and two were opened to outside methods, on the rule that hooks with no method are not built: structure serves the solver, openness is a promise to others, and a promise costs whether or not anyone uses it. Where a choice belongs is [J17 Decision placement](judgment.md#j17-decision-placement).

**What it is not.** Not a prohibition of strategy: the quality of choices is real work, and a system leaving every choice to a fixed default has not escaped choosing. Not a second decider hidden in the mechanical part, which would be a choice with no owner. And the test detects leakage from solver into problem, not the converse; passing it says nothing about whether the cut is useful.

**When to reach for it.** When an "optimization" changes results, or an extension point is proposed with nothing to put there.

**Signs you are violating it.** Results depend on evaluation order, on the number of workers or on which policy ran. A tie-break rule is specified though nobody relies on it. Policies validated against the facts when they were asked, not when they answer. Every decision point pluggable and none plugged.

**Worked application: a query planner.** The fixed derivation is the query's meaning, the equivalences rules may use, the properties consumers require and which plans are legal. The choices are join order, join algorithms, sorting or hashing, and how much of the space to explore. Vary join order and algorithm: the result must not change. A query with a row limit and no ordering fails, since different plans return different rows. Either the problem leaves the rows free (the user relies on "any N rows", and the system says so), or the user relies on an order the query does not state, an ordering hidden in what was meant to be control, and the fix belongs in the query's meaning, not in a planner rule. Openness is sized apart: opening join order to user hints is a promise, made when users have hints to give, and a hint is validated against the current schema and statistics when used, not when written.

## R7 Coupled or independent

**Question.** Are these sub-problems independent, nearly independent, or coupled through a shared resource, a global constraint or an objective that does not separate?

**Consequence.** Independent: expand and share. Nearly independent (within a time window, or in aggregate): expand, with the coupling carried as a fact at each sub-problem's boundary. Coupled: recursion organizes the coupled problem, and the coupling is stated, never assumed away. Where parts must run concurrently while exchanging, the unit is a joint execution. A proof of global feasibility over the whole decomposition is a manufactured requirement; admission of each step against current facts remains. Where a step is a one-shot external effect, it is not expanded. Where only one party decides, the recursion of agreement among parties does not start. A fact only a remote party knows is observed, not derived (R3).

**Why.** Pretending independence gives wrong answers silently; demanding proof of global feasibility gives a mechanism larger than the problem ([above](#when-recursion-is-the-form-and-when-it-is-not)). Example from the source history (objective-bound; [cases.md](cases.md), P3, P4 and P6): the first framing delegated "portions of both responsibility and resource capacity" down a tree of workers. The owner replaced delegation by a joint decision because a tree could not carry the coupling: "An implementation may be well suited to taking responsibility for a large fused region while being poorly suited to node-by-node invocation" (the owner of the source history, September 2026; `cases.md` P4). The workspace's last control round removed the claim that the tree guarantees global feasibility and kept admission against current facts.

Carrying a coupling as a fact means the sub-problem's description or facts say what it shares (this unknown, this resource, this boundary strip), and the procedure reads it when it chooses; it is not a negotiation protocol among sub-problems. Where agreement problems appear, ask first whether there is more than one party; in the source history, one decider per run removed nine at once ([reformulation.md](reformulation.md)).

**What it is not.** Not a reason to abandon expansion wherever there is coupling, nor to prove global feasibility. Not independence by drawing: Alexander's "In a single mental act you can only visualize a tree" warns that the tree may belong to the decomposer, not the problem.

**When to reach for it.** When parts share a resource, a variable, a deadline or an objective, or a proposal includes a global feasibility check.

**Signs you are violating it.** Each part optimized and the whole worse than a simpler plan. Parts blocking on each other's intermediate results. A global admission or reservation mechanism growing to protect an independence the problem lacks. Sub-problems whose boundaries move every step.

**Worked application: tiled post-processing, and a proof search.** A renderer applies screen-space effects (a blur of radius r; an occlusion pass sampling a neighbourhood) to a frame split into tiles. For one pass the tiles are nearly independent: each needs a halo of r pixels of its neighbours' input, a fact at its boundary. Chain k passes inside each tile and the halo grows to k times r; once it exceeds the tile, each tile needs its neighbours' intermediate results between passes, and the unit becomes the joint execution of the passes over the frame (every tile advances one pass, with an exchange between passes), not a tile carried through all passes alone. Reach per step times the number of steps decides whether a block-local run is a problem by itself, which is the source history's limit over partitioned domains in another vocabulary. In a proof search, two subgoals mentioning one metavariable are coupled, since proving one fixes the unknown for the other; Kowalski's subgoals that share variables are solved "compatibly". The shared metavariable is named in both goals, and the procedure works first on the goal that will fix it, or backtracks; it neither pretends independence nor demands up front a proof that some assignment satisfies all goals.

## Derived, not stored: what the history shows

An example, condensed from one history and told in full in [cases.md](cases.md) (P3 to P6, P13 to P16). Its form is enduring; the condition under which it worked is contextual; its content (a declarative array language, one decider per run) is objective-bound.

Over about four weeks, what recursed changed several times, always in one direction.

| Stage | What recursed | Where the recursion lived |
|---|---|---|
| The first framing (P3) | A worker holding responsibility and resource capacity, refining into sub-workers of the same kind | A logical tree that "represents responsibility, delegation, and resource ownership"; identity was a place in the tree |
| A control problem (P4) | A coupled decision (which region, who, which implementation, which resources, when) restated at each level | Regions named as restrictions of the program, so identity came from the shared representation, "never from the worker tree itself" |
| The cursor (P5a) | One loop over a bounded region | A resident runtime object with state, lifecycle and mailbox, carrying the identity the region lacked |
| Policy and handoff (P5b) | A one-off decision handing on responsibility, authority and knowledge together | Continuations, not nested calls: "Nobody waits for a lower level to return" |
| Two machines (P6) | A control request decomposed into requests of the same shape and smaller scope | The control side only, with levels corresponding "to problem decomposition, not node, process, MPI-rank, resource-pool, or authorization-tree levels" |
| A demand graph (P15) | Goals and the interests between them | A finite graph held by one owner; the software built a future per goal, a task map and a dependency graph between tasks |
| A derivation (P16) | Need on a piece of work becoming need on its producers | A relation evaluated on each event; what is owed "is derived, never stored"; the word "recursion" left the design |

What recursed moved from a unit that holds (a worker with capacity, a cursor with state) to a unit that decides (a policy applied once) to a function that is evaluated. Each step removed structure that existed only because the previous unit persisted: once the control was a one-off activation, eleven of the cursor specification's twenty-five invariants had "no object left to hold of".

Earlier, the system's first concrete candidate had shown one form for each recursion it carried. The problem's expansion was heap data on one thread, each node embedding its continuation, so a suspended parent held "no thread, task, or slot another node needs", and its sub-problem identity was a key in a lookup, reuse happening "by the demand index joining nodes, whose key is the goal itself". The construction of a computation was flattened into one record with provenance, no record per level. Execution responsibility rode on the operating system's own process hierarchy, whose resource accounting charges a child to its ancestors. None needed a runtime structure of its own. And the recursion of agreement among deciders never started, because there was one decider.

In the end state four things were stored, each because it crosses a real boundary: durable progress, work in flight, choices adopted and not yet carried out, and the facts the executing nodes report. What is owed, its names, who shares it, and whether to wait and on what were derived each time. One procedure still answered every kind of owed work; sharing was stronger, since any party could name the work; entering a run, taking one over and starting one became one path.

The methodology's reading: the runtime holds what crosses a real boundary, and the history moved recursion out of the runtime's ontology into the problem's derivation, under R3's condition (the facts the derivation needs can be read back). The record names what the move cost: the explicit parent-to-child edge; deduplication by key between unequal demands, which was never offered; straight-line code for multi-stage decisions; and lifting at run time, which was never built. Where the condition does not hold, ask which facts are durable and derive over them; do not bring the tree back.

## The atomic case and lifting

The first framing of the source history wanted the vocabulary of atomic cases open at run time: an opaque realization could be reopened into sub-workers, and a stable recurring subtree lifted into a new primitive, which the owner called "recursive closure" (the owner of the source history, September 2026; `cases.md` P3). It was never built. A survey of the workspace's generations found that wherever lifting existed it was a fixed lowering made at design time, and that "No reviewed generation supported discovering a recurring subtree and re-encapsulating it" (agents' text of the source history; `cases.md` P3). What survived is procedural: a new implementation able to handle a larger region is produced away from the running computation and admitted to the candidates the solver chooses among, and rewriting an indivisible operation into smaller ones takes effect only in a new version of the program. Whether the open vocabulary was dropped or deferred, the record does not say. (Example; the form enduring, the particular atom objective-bound.)

The literature names the ways lifting loses and is silent on where it is done; Soar's chunking lifts during problem solving. Lifting is a bet that a sub-structure recurs and that its internals will not be needed, and the bet loses three ways. The internals were needed: Sutton, Precup and Singh show an option treated as "an indivisible, opaque unit" doing worse than the same option interrupted with knowledge of the whole (over 200 time steps against 121 in their example), and warn that "there is no guarantee that multi-step options will reduce the overall expense of planning". The local optimum was not the global one (Dietterich). The repertoire outgrew the matching: the macro-operator tradition's "utility problem", named and not sized. Lifting also changes expressive power: Erol, Hendler and Nau show that a library of decomposition methods makes planning more expressive than primitive operators and, once unordered non-primitive tasks may interleave, undecidable. None of these sources says lifting is wrong or free, and none gives a criterion for when it pays.

The methodology's answer is a procedure with a condition, not a criterion. Where the solver's control or the fixed meaning must hold for a run, lift outside the live solving; the history's placement there followed from its fixed program, and it records no lifting performed under the rule. Elsewhere lifting during the solve is a design choice, made with its record and with the version of the meaning each answer used. The history assigns the finding of candidates to the slow loop and has no instance of how it is done; the literature's account is Amarel's, whose macro-steps "act as well-chosen lemmas" and are found in a representation in which the critical "narrows" are visible, by observing executions. Admit each new primitive as a candidate the same procedure chooses among, so the solver's control is unchanged and the closure the first framing wanted survives as a property of the representation (new primitives re-enter the same procedure), not of the running solver.

Moving the atom has two effects worth recording. It changes identities, so what was shared under the old atom is re-examined under the new one (R2). And it moves a line of ownership: what was inside another party's opaque unit may become the formulation's to define, or the reverse, and the record of the change says which ([reformulation.md](reformulation.md), the record).

## Sharing: the three conditions and the failure that breaks them

Query optimizers, build systems, incremental computation and hierarchical reinforcement learning state the same three conditions for sharing sub-problems, each in its own terms. A name derived from the problem alone: a key, a goal with its required properties, a group of equivalent expressions, a parameterized sub-task such as Dietterich's `Navigate(t)`, whose parameter lets one sub-solution serve two callers. Purity: the answer is a function of the name and of the answers to its named dependencies; Skyframe's documentation calls a read outside a requested dependency "an unregistered dependency" that "can cause incorrect incremental builds", and Salsa "assumes that `your_program` is a purely deterministic function of its inputs". And a record of what the answer depended on, so that reuse and invalidation are decidable: traces, a dependency graph, dependencies declared after looking at earlier results.

The failure that breaks them is always the same: an input the name does not mention. An unregistered file read, a physical property left out of an optimization goal, a state variable left out of a key; in each, the memo returns an answer computed under other conditions, and nothing reports it, because the mechanism is working as designed and the name is what is wrong.

The key is therefore where a sub-problem's independence is defined. A richer key makes sub-problems more independent and shares less; a poorer key shares more and is sometimes wrong. The problem's structure sets the trade, and R2's content test keeps it honest. A memo that makes reuse cheapest can also discard the intermediate names that let a change be recognized as not mattering: deep constructive traces, which look only at transitive inputs, "cannot support early cutoff".

Where identity is discovered during solving, the memo itself is revised: Cascades merges two groups found to be one; Shake adds dependencies after seeing earlier results; Skyframe restarts a function when a new dependency appears. Each treats an incomplete name as normal and has a repair path. A design that assumes its cuts are fixed before solving begins should say why that holds for its problem.

Sharing comes in three kinds, which the source history handled by one order (example, enduring): work in flight, found by looking up what is being produced and waiting on it; results, counted as coverage once shown complete; and reusable structures, reused by equality of description. In each case: use what exists, else wait on what is coming, else propose. That order needs no registry of sub-problems, which resolves the apparent conflict between sharing (two parties must name the same sub-problem) and refusing a registry: identity by description plus a lookup in a ledger that exists anyway for another reason (the record of what was handed to executors) does the registry's work.

Two last points. Identity can be shared while representation is not: in an earlier form of the same history, a region was named in a global space so two policies could refer to one sub-problem while each held its own representation of it, the handoff being the only conversion. And overlap is not a sharing problem (R2).
